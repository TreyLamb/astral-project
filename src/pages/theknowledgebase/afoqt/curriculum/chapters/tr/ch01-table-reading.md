# Table Reading — the method

**40 questions. 7 minutes. 10.5 seconds each.** No calculator, no straight edge, and a grid
roughly 33 columns wide by 33 rows deep.

This is the best-value subtest on the whole test and it is worth knowing why before you spend
a minute on it:

- It feeds **all three rated composites** — Pilot, CSO and ABM. Nothing else except Math
  Knowledge reaches that far.
- It contains **no knowledge**. You cannot be under-taught here. There is no fact to forget on
  test day, no formula to misremember, nothing that goes stale.
- It is therefore **pure trainable speed**, which means practice converts to score more
  directly here than anywhere else on the test.

The Air Force's own instructions warn that *many people do not finish* this subtest. That is
the design, not a failure. Your job is not to finish gracefully — it is to be accurate and fast
enough that you finish more of it than the person next to you, and to mark every remaining
answer before time is called.

---

## The grid, and the one fact that decides your score

> **X runs across the TOP and ASCENDS left to right.**
> **Y runs down the LEFT and DESCENDS top to bottom.**

Read that second line again. On every graph you have drawn since middle school, Y gets **bigger**
as you go up and this table agrees — but you are reading *down* a printed page, and going down
this table means Y gets **smaller**. The top row is the largest Y. The bottom row is the most
negative.

| | going right → | going down ↓ |
|---|---|---|
| **X** | increases | *(unchanged)* |
| **Y** | *(unchanged)* | **decreases** |

Every instinct you have will fight this for about the first thirty questions you practise, and
then it will stop fighting. That transition is the single highest-value thing in this chapter,
and the only way to get it is repetition — which is exactly what the drill below is for.

**Why it matters so much:** a Y-axis flip does not produce an obviously silly answer. It
produces a real value, printed in the real table, sitting in the answer options waiting for you.
Every one of the five options is a genuine value from the same grid. There is no such thing as a
number that "looks wrong" here.

---

## The second fact: the values change gradually

Take the Air Force's own sample table:

| | −3 | −2 | −1 | 0 | +1 | +2 | +3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **+3** | 25 | 26 | 28 | 30 | 31 | 32 | 33 |
| **+2** | 26 | 28 | 30 | 32 | 33 | 34 | 35 |
| **+1** | 27 | 29 | 31 | 33 | 35 | 36 | 37 |
| **0** | 29 | 30 | 32 | 34 | 36 | 37 | 38 |
| **−1** | 30 | 32 | 33 | 35 | 37 | 38 | 40 |
| **−2** | 31 | 33 | 34 | 36 | 38 | 39 | 41 |
| **−3** | 32 | 34 | 35 | 37 | 39 | 40 | 42 |

Every step right adds one or two. Every step *down* adds one or two. Nothing jumps.

That single property is what makes this subtest hard, and it has a consequence you need to
internalise before you start drilling:

> **A slipped row costs you one point on the page, so you cannot feel the mistake.**
> The official sample's first question answers **33** and offers you **30, 33, 34, 35, 36**.
> Four of those five are neighbours of the right cell.

So there is no sanity check available to you. Not "does this number look about right" — they all
look about right. Not "is this in the ballpark" — they are all in the ballpark. The *only* thing
that separates a right answer from a wrong one is whether you tracked the row and the column
exactly. That is why the discipline below is the entire skill, and why the drills in this
chapter include one where all five options are immediate neighbours.

⚠ Do not try to exploit the gradient. The increments are irregular — the sample's top row runs
1, 2, 2, 1, 1, 1 while the row below it runs 2, 2, 2, 1, 1, 1 — so there is no formula, and a
number you estimated is a number you guessed.

## The anchor method

Do **not** count from the edge of the table. The edge is up to 32 cells away from where you are
going, and the whole subtest is one long exercise in not slipping a row.

Anchor on the middle instead:

1. **Read both coordinates and note their signs first.** `X = −11, Y = +6` is *left of centre,
   above centre* before it is anything else. Knowing the quadrant means a mistake of 20 rows
   announces itself immediately.
2. **Find the zero column.** `X = 0` sits in the middle of the top row. Positive X is to its
   right, negative X to its left. Count out from there, never in from the edge.
3. **Find the zero row.** `Y = 0` sits in the middle of the left column. Positive Y is **above**
   it, negative Y **below** it.
4. **Converge.** Come down the column and across the row toward each other rather than
   travelling the full length of either. From a centre anchor the longest journey on the grid is
   16 cells, not 32 — you have halved the distance over which you can drift.
5. **Verify before you answer.** Glance left from the cell you landed on and confirm the row
   label. One second, and it catches the error that costs you the question.

Step 5 is not optional padding. It is the cheapest insurance on the test.

## Tracking without a straight edge

You are not permitted a ruler, a card or the edge of your answer sheet. So the two long moves
have different risks and want different techniques:

- **Moving down a column** is the safer of the two. Columns are separated by more space than
  rows are, so drifting sideways is hard. Use your finger or the cursor and go.
- **Moving across a row** is where people slip. Text rows sit close together, and after fifteen
  columns the eye quietly picks up the row above or below. If you must travel a long way
  horizontally, break it into two hops with a landmark in the middle — the zero column is
  always there.

Practical order: **column first, row second.** Locate the X column at the top, come down it, and
let the *row* be the thing you arrive at rather than the thing you travel along. That trades the
risky horizontal scan for the safer vertical one.

## The five ways to get one wrong

The wrong answers on this subtest are not random numbers. They are the exact cells you land on
when you make one specific mistake, which is why this tool tells you *which* mistake you made
after every drill rather than just how many you missed.

| Error | What it looks like | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Y read as ascending** | You counted the Y axis upward from the bottom. Lands you on the mirrored row. | The anchor. Positive Y is *above* centre. |
| **Slipped a row** | Off by one vertically, almost always after a long horizontal scan. Costs you one or two points, so it looks completely reasonable. | Verify the row label before answering. |
| **Slipped a column** | Off by one horizontally, usually after a long vertical scan. Same — a near-miss you cannot feel. | Column first, row second. |
| **Swapped X and Y** | You looked up (Y, X). The pair is always **(X, Y)**. | Say the coordinates out loud in your head: "X first." |
| **Dropped a minus sign** | Read `−11` as `11` and went the wrong way from centre. | Note both signs *before* you start moving. |

If your post-drill breakdown says the same error three times, that is not bad luck — that is the
one habit to fix before your next run.

## The near-miss drill

One drill in this chapter is deliberately the worst case: every one of the five options is a
cell immediately touching the answer, so the slate reads something like **088 089 090 091 092**.

It feels unfair, and it is the most useful practice here. There is no technique that rescues a
badly tracked row — only a well-tracked one — so this is where you find out whether your method
actually works or whether you have been getting away with near-enough. If you are missing these
and hitting the ordinary lookups, the problem is your row tracking, not your speed.

## Pacing — the actual subtest

Seven minutes for forty questions. Write these checkpoints on your scratch paper before the
subtest starts:

| Clock elapsed | You should be at |
|---|---|
| 1:45 | question 10 |
| 3:30 | question 20 |
| 5:15 | question 30 |
| 7:00 | question 40 |

Three rules follow from that table:

1. **Ten seconds is the budget.** If a question is going badly at five seconds, it is not going
   to come good at fifteen. Mark your best guess and move.
2. **There is no penalty for a wrong answer.** A blank scores exactly the same as a wrong answer
   and worse than a lucky guess, so a blank is never correct behaviour. In the last fifteen
   seconds, stop reading and mark every remaining question.
3. **Accuracy first, early.** The questions do not get harder as they go, so a rushed error on
   question 4 costs exactly as much as a rushed error on question 40 — and unlike question 40,
   you had time for it.

---

## Before you move on

- [ ] Down the table means Y gets **smaller**, and you no longer have to think about it.
- [ ] You know the options are near-misses, so "that looks about right" is never evidence.
- [ ] You anchor on the zero row and zero column, never on the edge.
- [ ] You find the column first and come down it.
- [ ] You glance at the row label before you commit to an answer.
- [ ] You can name your own most frequent error from the last drill's breakdown.
- [ ] You know where you should be at 3:30, and you never leave a blank.
