# Percent, percent change and interest

Percent questions on the AFOQT are almost never about arithmetic. They are about **which
number is the base** — the thing you are taking a percent *of*. Get the base right and the
rest is one multiplication.

One relationship carries the whole chapter:

> **part = percent × whole**

Three questions come out of it depending on which piece is missing.

---

## 1. Finding the part

`p% of n` → convert the percent to a decimal and multiply. "Of" means multiply.

`25% of 340` → `0.25 × 340 = **85**`

> **The trap:** the answer slate carries `8.5`, `850` and `8500`. All of them are the right
> digits with the decimal point in the wrong place. `25% = 0.25`, two places, always.

Useful shortcuts under time pressure:

| Percent | Do this instead |
|---|---|
| 10% | move the decimal one place left |
| 5% | half of 10% |
| 25% | divide by 4 |
| 20% | divide by 5 |
| 50% | halve it |
| 15% | 10% + half of that |

---

## 2. Finding the whole

Same relationship, different unknown — so it is a **division**, not a multiplication.

> 60 is 25% of what number?
> `whole = part ÷ percent = 60 ÷ 0.25 = **240**`

> **The trap:** reflexively taking 25% *of* 60 and answering 15. The stem said 60 already
> *is* the 25%. Whenever the words "of what number" appear, you are dividing.

Sanity check: 25% is a quarter, so the whole must be about four times the part. 240 passes;
15 fails instantly.

---

## 3. Percent change

> **percent change = (change ÷ ORIGINAL) × 100**

The word ORIGINAL is doing all the work. It is the number you started at, not the one you
ended at.

> A quantity falls from 400 to 200.
> Change = 200. `200 ÷ **400** = 50%` decrease.

> **The trap:** dividing by the new value. `200 ÷ 200 = 100%`, and "100%" is on the slate.
> Dividing by the wrong base is the single most common error on this question type.

This is also why **a 50% drop needs a 100% rise to get back**. The base changed. That asymmetry
is worth internalising — it is the same idea the next section runs on.

---

## 4. Successive discounts

> **Percentages never add.**

A $500 jacket is marked down 30%, then the sale price is reduced a further 20%.

1. After 30% off: `500 × 0.70 = $350`
2. After a further 20% off **that**: `350 × 0.80 = **$280**`

> **The trap:** treating it as 50% off, giving $250. That answer is on the slate and it is
> wrong by $30, because the second discount applies to the *reduced* price, not the original.

The overall discount here is 44%, not 50%. Two discounts of `a%` and `b%` combine as
`(1 − a)(1 − b)`, which is always *less* total discount than `a + b`.

Same machinery, opposite direction: a 10% raise followed by a 10% pay cut leaves you at
`1.10 × 0.90 = 0.99` — down 1%, not level.

---

## 5. Simple interest

> **I = P × r × t** — principal, rate as a decimal, time in years.

$5,000 at 6% for 4 years → `5000 × 0.06 × 4 = **$1,200** in interest`.

Two things to keep separate:

- **Simple interest is charged on the original principal every year.** It never earns interest
  on itself. Compound interest does, and compounding is *not* on the AFOQT's confirmed item
  list — if a question mentions interest, read it as simple unless it says otherwise.
- **Interest is not the balance.** Interest earned is $1,200; the account holds $6,200. Both
  numbers will be on the slate, and only the stem says which one is wanted.

---

## Before you move on

Read the stem twice for one thing only: **what is the base?**

| The question says | The base is |
|---|---|
| "…% of X" | X |
| "…is …% of what number" | the unknown — divide |
| "percent increase/decrease" | the ORIGINAL value |
| "a further …% off" | the already-reduced price |
| "simple interest" | the original principal, every year |
