# Exponents, radicals and scientific notation

Three rules do nearly all the work, and nearly every wrong answer is one of the three applied
in the wrong place.

| Operation | What happens to the exponents |
|---|---|
| **multiply** like bases | **ADD** them |
| **divide** like bases | **SUBTRACT** them |
| **power of a power** | **MULTIPLY** them |

Say them in that order once and the rest of the chapter is bookkeeping.

---

## 1. Multiplying and dividing

`(4x³)(6x⁵)` → coefficients **multiply** (`4 × 6 = 24`), exponents **add** (`3 + 5 = 8`)
→ `**24x⁸**`

`18x⁷ ÷ 3x²` → coefficients **divide** (`18 ÷ 3 = 6`), exponents **subtract** (`7 − 2 = 5`)
→ `**6x⁵**`

> **The trap:** doing the same thing to the coefficients as to the exponents. The coefficients
> multiply and divide normally — only the *exponents* add and subtract. `24x¹⁵` and `10x⁸` are
> both among the answer choices for the first one.

---

## 2. Power of a power

`(5x⁴)³`

The outer power hits **everything** inside: `5³ = 125` and `x^(4×3) = x¹²` → `**125x¹²**`

> **The trap:** leaving the coefficient alone and answering `5x¹²`. It is the most common miss
> in this chapter. If it is inside the parentheses, it gets raised too.

Same rule with a quotient: `(2/3)² = 4/9` — top *and* bottom.

---

## 3. Zero and negative exponents

> **Anything nonzero to the power 0 is 1.** Not 0.

That follows directly from the division rule: `x⁵ ÷ x⁵ = x⁰`, and anything divided by itself
is 1.

> **A negative exponent means RECIPROCAL, not negative.**

`3⁻² = 1/3² = 1/9`. The answer is positive. `(2/5)⁻³ = (5/2)³ = 125/8` — flip, then raise.

> **The trap:** `−9` and `−1/9` are both among the answer choices. A negative exponent never changes the
> *sign* of the answer; it moves the term across the fraction bar.

Put the two together: `(7x⁴)(x⁻⁴) = 7x⁰ = **7**`. The x is gone, but the 7 is not.

---

## 4. Radicals

Simplifying `√n` means pulling out the largest **perfect square** factor and taking *its*
root.

`√300` → `300 = 100 × 3` → `√100 × √3 = **10√3**`

> **The trap:** answering `100√3`. What comes out of the radical is the **square root** of the
> square factor, not the factor. Check by squaring your answer: `(10√3)² = 100 × 3 = 300` ✓.

Perfect squares worth recognising instantly: 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144, 169,
196, 225, 256, 400, 625.

Two rules that hold and one that does not:

- `√a × √b = √(ab)` ✓
- `√a ÷ √b = √(a/b)` ✓
- `√(a + b) ≠ √a + √b` ✗ — `√(9+16) = 5`, but `3 + 4 = 7`.

A radical is also a fractional exponent: `√x = x^(1/2)`, `∛x = x^(1/3)`. That is occasionally
the fastest route through a messy expression.

---

## 5. Scientific notation

One nonzero digit before the decimal point, times a power of ten.

`347000 = **3.47 × 10⁵**` — the point moved five places left, so the exponent is +5.
`0.00347 = **3.47 × 10⁻³**` — the point moved three places right, so the exponent is −3.

Big number → positive exponent. Small number → negative exponent.

> **The trap:** `34.7 × 10⁴` is the same *number* but it is **not scientific notation**,
> because the mantissa must be at least 1 and less than 10. That option will be among the answer choices,
> and it is testing the definition rather than the arithmetic.

Multiplying: multiply the mantissas, add the exponents, then renormalise if the mantissa left
the 1–10 range.

`(3 × 10⁴)(4 × 10³) = 12 × 10⁷ = **1.2 × 10⁸**`

---

## Before you move on

1. Multiply → add exponents. Divide → subtract. Power of a power → multiply.
2. The outer power hits the coefficient too.
3. `x⁰ = 1`; `x⁻ⁿ = 1/xⁿ`, and neither one makes anything negative.
4. Out of a radical comes the *root* of the square factor.
5. Scientific notation means exactly one digit before the point.
