# Main idea and author intent

Two related but distinct question types live in this chapter: what is the passage's main idea,
and what would the author most likely agree with. Both require understanding the passage as a
whole rather than pulling one detail out of it - but they ask for different things.

---

## 1. Main idea - the thesis, not a true supporting detail

A main-idea question asks for the single claim every paragraph in the passage is working to
support. The classic wrong answer here is not false - it is a real detail from the passage that
is simply too narrow to be the *main* point.

Take a passage arguing that sustainment (supply and logistics) is a decisive, underrated part of
military planning. "Pre-positioning stock ties up money and space" is a true sentence straight
from that passage - but it is one supporting detail among several, not the passage's actual
thesis. The main idea has to be broad enough to explain why the passage included every one of its
paragraphs, not just one of them.

**The test:** for each answer choice, ask "does the ENTIRE passage support this, or just one part
of it?" A choice that only one paragraph supports is a detail wearing a main-idea costume.

---

## 2. Author agreement - what follows from the argument, not what sounds reasonable

An author-agreement question asks which statement the author would endorse, based on the
argument actually made in the passage - not which statement is simply true, or reasonable-sounding
in general. This is a subtler trap than it looks: several wrong choices are often perfectly
sensible claims that a reasonable person might hold, but that this specific author's specific
argument does not actually support or even address at all.

If a passage argues that a country's basing agreements require continuous diplomatic maintenance
rather than one-time securing, the author would agree with a statement close to that claim - not
with a generic true statement about international relations that the passage never actually made
an argument for or against.

**The test:** find the sentence or paragraph in the passage that the correct choice restates or
follows directly from. If you cannot point to a specific place in the text supporting a choice, it
is very likely a distractor, however reasonable it sounds taken on its own.

---

## Both types share one discipline: go back to the text

Neither question type rewards a strong general impression of "what the passage was about." Before
selecting an answer, find the specific sentence, or handful of sentences, that justifies it. If you
cannot, reconsider the choice - a correct main-idea or author-agreement answer is always traceable
to something the passage actually says, not to a plausible inference layered on top of a vague memory.

---

## Before you move on

| Signal | Do this |
|---|---|
| A choice is true but only about one paragraph | It is a supporting detail, not the main idea - keep looking |
| A choice sounds reasonable in general | Check whether the passage's specific argument actually supports it - reasonable is not the same as textually supported |
| You cannot point to the sentence backing a choice | Reconsider it - both question types require a traceable textual basis |
