# Weather and performance

**Weather is one of the four sections the official AFOQT Knowledge Check is divided into**, so it
carries real weight. Two of the ten sampled official items are here, and both are common-sense
questions with a physical reason behind them:

> *"Which weather condition is best for smooth flight?"* → cool and dry
> *"It is best to land ___"* → into the wind

---

## Pressure systems: everything follows from up or down

| | Air motion | Rotation (northern hemisphere) | Weather |
|---|---|---|---|
| **High pressure** | **Descending** | Clockwise, outward | Fair — sinking air warms and dries, so cloud cannot form |
| **Low pressure** | **Rising** | Counter-clockwise, inward | Poor — rising air cools, water condenses, you get weather |

That is the whole logic. Sinking air = no cloud. Rising air = cloud, and often rain.

Which is also why **cool and dry air flies smoothest**: it has neither the moisture to make cloud
nor the heating that drives convection.

**Layers:** all weather is in the **troposphere**. The **tropopause** is the lid on top of it, and
the **jet stream** lives there — a narrow ribbon of very strong wind flowing generally west to
east. Above that, the **stratosphere** is stable and dry.

---

## Fronts

| Front | Slope and speed | Weather |
|---|---|---|
| **Cold** | Steep, **fast** | Brief and violent — cumulus, thunderstorms — then rapid clearing |
| **Warm** | Shallow, **slow** | Layered stratus, steady rain, hours of poor visibility |
| **Stationary** | Neither air mass advances | Prolonged, unchanging conditions |
| **Occluded** | A cold front **overtakes** a warm front, lifting the warm air off the surface | Mixed characteristics of both |

Cold front = short and nasty. Warm front = long and grey. That contrast is the examinable part.

## Clouds

Three shape words and two modifiers, which combine:

- **Cirrus** — high, wispy, made of **ice crystals**. Often the first hint of an approaching warm
  front.
- **Stratus** — flat featureless layers, indicating **stable** air.
- **Cumulus** — heaped and puffy, indicating **unstable** air and **turbulence**.
- **nimbus** — this cloud is **producing precipitation**.
- **alto-** — this cloud is at **middle** altitude (roughly 6,500–20,000 ft).

So *cumulonimbus* is a heaped cloud that is raining: a thunderstorm.

## Fog, by how it forms

| Fog | Formed by |
|---|---|
| **Radiation** | Clear, calm nights — the ground cools the air above it to its dew point |
| **Advection** | Moist air moving **horizontally** over a colder surface. Needs wind to persist |
| **Upslope** | Moist air pushed **up** terrain, cooling by expansion |
| **Steam** | Cold dry air moving over much **warmer water** |

The **dew point** is the temperature at which air becomes saturated. When temperature and dew
point converge to within a few degrees, expect cloud, fog or precipitation.

A **temperature inversion** is a layer where temperature *rises* with height — it traps haze and
pollutants underneath.

## Thunderstorms and turbulence

Three stages, and the middle one is the dangerous one:

1. **Cumulus** — building; **updraughts only**.
2. **Mature** — **the most dangerous stage**, when updraughts and downdraughts coexist. The tell
   is that **precipitation reaches the ground**, which means a downdraught now exists alongside
   the updraught, and the shear between them is what destroys aircraft.
3. **Dissipating** — **downdraughts** dominate as the cell rains itself out.

Named hazards:

- **Wind shear** — a sudden change of wind speed or direction over a short distance.
- **Microburst** — a small, intense column of sinking air with a violent surface outflow.
- **Clear air turbulence** — at altitude near the jet stream, with **no cloud to warn you**.
- **Convective turbulence** — rising columns from unevenly heated ground (the afternoon bumps).
- **Mechanical turbulence** — wind disrupted by terrain, buildings, trees.

## Icing

| Ice | Looks like | Forms from |
|---|---|---|
| **Clear** | Smooth, transparent, **heavy**, hard to shed | Large supercooled droplets that flow back before freezing |
| **Rime** | White, rough, **brittle**, opaque | Small droplets freezing on contact |
| **Mixed** | Both at once | Both |

> ⚠ **Frost must be removed before flight** — and *not* because of its weight, which is
> negligible. It is the **roughness**. Frost disrupts the airflow enough to stop the wing lifting
> at all on the takeoff roll.

## ⭐ Density altitude

**Density altitude is pressure altitude corrected for temperature, and it is the number that
governs how the aircraft actually performs.**

Three altitudes, kept straight:

- **True altitude** — the real height above mean sea level.
- **Pressure altitude** — what the altimeter reads with **29.92** set.
- **Density altitude** — pressure altitude corrected for **non-standard temperature**.

**Hot, high and humid all raise density altitude**, and thin air degrades everything at once: the
wing makes less lift, the engine draws less air, and the propeller has less to bite on. The
result is a **longer takeoff roll and a reduced rate of climb**.

> ⚠ **Humid air is LESS dense than dry air.** This is the one people get backwards, so it is the
> one that gets asked. A water molecule is lighter than the nitrogen or oxygen it displaces, so
> moist air weighs *less* — which is exactly why humidity hurts performance rather than helping.
