# Airspace, right of way and altitudes

The most rule-shaped chapter in the subtest, and therefore the most reliably scoreable. None of
it requires understanding. All of it is lookup, and the rules do not change.

---

## The classes, as shapes

| Class | What it is | Requirement to enter |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | 18,000 ft up to FL600 | **IFR only** — no VFR at all |
| **B** | The busiest airports, tiered like an upside-down wedding cake | An explicit **clearance** |
| **C** | Moderately busy airports with approach radar | Two-way radio contact established |
| **D** | An airport with an **operating control tower** — that is the whole criterion | Two-way radio contact |
| **E** | General controlled airspace filling the gaps | Nothing, for VFR |
| **G** | Uncontrolled — ATC provides no separation | Nothing |

Two related items:

- **Mode C veil** — the 30-nautical-mile ring around a Class B airport inside which an
  altitude-reporting **transponder** is required.
- **ATIS** is the looping recorded broadcast of airport weather. **CTAF** is the frequency for
  self-announcing at a field with no tower. **UNICOM** is a private advisory station.

## Special use airspace

| Type | Can you fly through it? |
|---|---|
| **Prohibited** | **Never** |
| **Restricted** | Only with permission — contains hazards like live artillery |
| **Warning** | Same hazards as restricted, but over **international water** (from 3 nm out) |
| **MOA** | **Yes** — military training area, but VFR traffic may legally transit |
| **Alert** | Yes — just a warning of high training volume; everyone is equally responsible |
| **TFR** | Short-notice closure around a fire, a stadium or a VIP movement |

The MOA is the one to remember, because it is the exception: it *sounds* the most restrictive and
is actually the most permissive of the military areas.

## VFR weather minimums

The standard case — controlled airspace below 10,000 feet:

> **3 statute miles visibility**
> **500 feet below, 1,000 feet above, 2,000 feet horizontally** from cloud.

A way to hold it: **1-3-5-2** — 1,000 above, 3 miles, 500 below, 2,000 horizontal.

Two exceptions worth knowing:

- **Class G, daytime, below 1,200 ft AGL** — drops to **1 statute mile and clear of clouds**.
- **Class B** — **3 statute miles and clear of clouds**, with *no* distance-from-cloud
  requirement, because ATC is separating every aircraft in there anyway.

**Special VFR** is a clearance permitting flight below the normal minimums in a control zone.

## Right of way — a fixed pecking order

The principle is **least manoeuvrable first**. Something that cannot get out of the way has the
right to stay where it is.

> **Distress → Balloon → Glider → Airship → Aeroplane / Rotorcraft**

A powered aeroplane gives way to essentially everything. Among powered aircraft, one that is
**towing or refuelling another** has right of way over the rest.

Three geometry rules:

| Situation | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Head on** | **Both** aircraft turn **right** |
| **Converging** (same category) | The aircraft on the **right** has right of way |
| **Overtaking** | The **overtaking** aircraft alters course to the **right** |
| **Both landing** | The **lower** aircraft has right of way — but you may not use that to cut in front of one already on final |

Notice that the answer is "right" in all three geometry cases. That is not a coincidence — it is
the same convention as the road in most of the world, and it makes the set easy to hold.

## ⭐ The hemispheric rule

Another rule that generates answers rather than a fact to recall. Above **3,000 feet AGL**, VFR
cruising altitude depends on your **magnetic course**:

| Magnetic course | Cruise at |
|---|---|
| **0° – 179°** (easterly) | **ODD** thousands **+ 500** — 3,500 · 5,500 · 7,500 |
| **180° – 359°** (westerly) | **EVEN** thousands **+ 500** — 4,500 · 6,500 · 8,500 |

**East is odd** is the hook. The whole point is to keep opposing traffic 1,000 feet apart.

Two details that get tested:

- It applies to your **course**, not your heading. A strong crosswind can have you pointing well
  off your course, and the rule follows the course.
- It only begins **above 3,000 feet above the surface**.
