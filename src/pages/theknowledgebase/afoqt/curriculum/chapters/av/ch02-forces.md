# The four forces and the wing

Two of the Air Force's own sample questions come straight out of this chapter:

> *"The rearward retarding force of airplane drag is opposed by ___"* → thrust
> *"The angle formed by the chord of an airfoil and the direction of the relative wind is called
> the ___"* → angle of attack

Both are vocabulary questions wearing physics clothing. You are not being asked to calculate
anything — you are being asked which word names which thing.

---

## The four forces, as two opposing pairs

| Force | Direction | Opposed by |
|---|---|---|
| **Lift** | Up | Weight |
| **Weight** | Down | Lift |
| **Thrust** | Forward | Drag |
| **Drag** | Rearward | Thrust |

In **straight-and-level unaccelerated flight**, lift equals weight and thrust equals drag. All
four in balance.

> ⚠ **A climb does not come from extra lift.** This is the most common wrong belief in the whole
> subject. In a steady climb, lift is actually slightly *less* than weight. What buys you the
> climb is **surplus thrust** — power you are not spending on going forward. A climb is a power
> problem, not a lift problem.

---

## The shape of a wing

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Airfoil** | Any surface shaped to make a useful force from moving air |
| **Chord line** | Straight line, leading edge to trailing edge |
| **Mean camber line** | Line drawn halfway between the upper and lower surfaces |
| **Camber** | The curvature. More camber = more lift at a given angle |
| **Relative wind** | The airflow as the wing meets it — always directly opposite the flight path |
| **Centre of pressure** | The point where all the lift is considered to act |
| **Planform** | The wing's shape seen from above |

---

## ⭐ The one confusion that matters most

This pair is the most reliable trap in Aviation Information. Both are angles, both involve the
chord line, and they mean completely different things.

| | Measured between | Changes in flight? |
|---|---|---|
| **Angle of ATTACK** | Chord line and the **relative wind** | **Yes** — constantly |
| **Angle of INCIDENCE** | Chord line and the **fuselage** | **No** — fixed when the aircraft is built |

**The tell is the relative wind.** If a question mentions it, the answer is angle of *attack*.

And a third, thrown in to make it worse: **pitch attitude** is the angle between the aircraft and
the *horizon*. You can be nose-high with a low angle of attack, or nose-low and fully stalled.
Attitude is against the horizon; angle of attack is against the air.

---

## Where lift comes from — both answers are correct

The AFOQT will accept either explanation, and it asks about both, so know both:

- **Bernoulli's principle** — air moving faster over the curved upper surface has *lower
  pressure*, and the pressure difference lifts the wing. (The **venturi effect** is the same idea
  in a constriction.)
- **Newton's third law** — the wing deflects a mass of air *downward* (**downwash**), and the
  equal and opposite reaction pushes the wing up.

They are two descriptions of one event, not competing theories.

---

## Drag comes in two families

This split is worth real attention, because the two behave in **opposite** ways with speed and
that opposition is examinable.

**Induced drag** — the unavoidable price of making lift. It comes from the wingtip vortices.
**Greatest at low speed**, because a slow wing needs a high angle of attack to hold you up.

**Parasite drag** — the cost of shoving the airframe through the air. **Grows with the square of
speed.** Three components:

- **Form drag** — from the *shape* of a component.
- **Skin friction drag** — from air rubbing along the *surface*.
- **Interference drag** — from two airflows *meeting*, typically at a wing root.

> Where the two curves cross is the speed of **best lift-to-drag ratio** — your best glide, and
> your maximum range.

---

## Three effects worth naming

- **Wingtip vortices** — high pressure under the wing escaping around the tip and spiralling.
  They are strongest when the aircraft is **heavy, slow and clean**, which is exactly takeoff and
  landing configuration, and is why wake turbulence separation exists.
- **Ground effect** — within about one wingspan of the surface, the ground interrupts those
  vortices, so **induced drag falls**. It is why an overloaded aircraft can stagger into the air
  and then refuse to climb out of it.
- **Boundary layer** — the thin band of air right against the skin, slowed by friction.

## Before you move on

- [ ] The four forces and what each opposes, instantly.
- [ ] A climb comes from surplus **thrust**, not extra lift.
- [ ] Angle of attack = chord line to **relative wind**, and it changes. Incidence is fixed.
- [ ] Induced drag is worst **slow**; parasite drag is worst **fast**.
- [ ] You can name form, skin friction and interference drag as the three parasite components.
- [ ] Ground effect *reduces induced drag*; winglets do the same thing by another means.
