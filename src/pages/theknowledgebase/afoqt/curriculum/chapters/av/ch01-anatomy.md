# Aircraft anatomy and the controls

**20 questions. 8 minutes. 24 seconds each.** Aviation Information feeds the **Pilot** and **ABM**
composites.

Start here, and start with why this subtest is worth more of your time than its twenty questions
suggest. Every other subtest measures something you already have — vocabulary, arithmetic,
pattern speed. This one measures something you were never taught, which means **a few evenings
of work moves this score more than the same hours anywhere else on the test**. There is no
aptitude component. It is a vocabulary test about aeroplanes.

The register is set by the Air Force's own sample questions, and it is lower than people expect:

> *"An aircraft's elevator functions to ___"* → change pitch
> *"The cowling is located ___"* → around the engine

Name the part, say what it does. That is the whole game.

## The three primary controls, and the three axes

This is the highest-value table in the chapter. Learn it as a unit — the three surfaces, the
three motions and the three axes go together, and the test will give you any one and ask for
another.

| Surface | Motion | Axis | Which way it runs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Aileron** | Roll | Longitudinal | Nose to tail |
| **Elevator** | Pitch | Lateral | Wingtip to wingtip |
| **Rudder** | Yaw | Vertical | Straight down through the aircraft |

Two memory hooks that hold up:

- The **elevator** *elevates* — it moves the nose up and down.
- The **rudder** is the one a boat has, and a boat's rudder swings the bow left and right. Same
  motion, same word.

> ⚠ **The rudder does not turn the aircraft.** Aileron rolls it into a bank; the bank is what
> turns it. Rudder only keeps the turn coordinated. Any option that says "the rudder turns the
> aircraft" is wrong, and it is offered often.

## Fixed versus moving

Half the confusion in this chapter is between a fixed surface and the moving one attached to it.
They are always named as a pair:

| Fixed | Moving part on it | What the fixed one does |
|---|---|---|
| **Vertical stabilizer** (the fin) | Rudder | Directional stability — keeps the nose pointed forward |
| **Horizontal stabilizer** (the tailplane) | Elevator | Longitudinal stability — keeps the nose from pitching |

A **stabilator** is the exception: one piece that pivots as a whole, doing the job of the
horizontal stabilizer and the elevator together. Because that makes it very powerful, it carries
an **antiservo tab** to deliberately add resistance so it does not feel twitchy.

> Tabs are a small confusion set worth ten seconds:
> - **Trim tab** — moves *opposite* the surface, holds it in place so you can let go.
> - **Antiservo tab** — moves *with* the surface, makes it feel heavier. Stabilators only.
> - **Servo tab** — moves *opposite*, and lets the airflow do the work of moving the surface.

## Secondary surfaces: lift devices and lift destroyers

Two groups, and they do opposite things.

**Add lift** (all on the wing):

- **Flaps** — trailing edge. Increase lift *and* drag. That extra drag is deliberate: it lets you
  come down steeply without speeding up.
- **Slats** — *leading* edge, extend forward. Delay the stall to a higher angle of attack.
- **Slots** — a fixed gap near the leading edge doing the same job with no moving parts.
- **Krueger flaps** — a leading-edge flap that folds down out of the *underside* of the wing.

**Destroy lift:**

- **Spoilers** — hinge up from the upper surface and break the airflow. Used in flight to descend
  or slow, and on landing to plant the weight on the wheels so the brakes bite.

> ⚠ **Flap versus slat is the pair that gets swapped.** Flap = trailing edge, adds lift and drag.
> Slat = leading edge, buys you a higher angle of attack. If a question mentions the leading edge,
> flaps are not the answer.

## Wing shape, and what each shape buys

| Feature | What it is | What it buys |
|---|---|---|
| **Dihedral** | Wings angled **up** from root to tip | Lateral stability — the aircraft rights itself |
| **Anhedral** | Wings angled **down** | Manoeuvrability at the cost of stability |
| **Sweep** | Wings angled rearward | Delays the drag rise near the speed of sound |
| **Washout** | Wing twisted so the tip has a lower angle than the root | The **root stalls first**, so the ailerons keep working |
| **Aspect ratio** | Span ÷ average chord | High (long, thin) = less induced drag, better glide |

Washout is worth understanding rather than memorising, because the reason is the answer: if the
*tip* stalled first you would lose roll control at the exact moment you needed it. Washout
guarantees the root lets go first. **Stall strips** do the same job by a different means.

## Adverse yaw

Roll into a turn and the aircraft initially yaws the *wrong way*. Here is why, and the why is what
gets asked:

1. The rising wing's aileron went down, so that wing is making **more lift**.
2. More lift means more **induced drag** on that same wing.
3. Extra drag on the outside wing pulls the nose *away* from the turn.

The fix is rudder in the direction of the turn. Designers reduce it with **differential
ailerons** (up-going aileron deflects further) or **Frise ailerons** (the raised aileron pokes its
nose into the airflow to add drag on the other side).

## Everything else you can point at

- **Fuselage** — the central body.
- **Empennage** — the whole tail assembly.
- **Cowling** — the removable cover *around the engine*.
- **Nacelle** — a streamlined housing carrying an engine away from the fuselage.
- **Fairing** — a non-structural cover fitted only to smooth airflow.
- **Winglet** — a wingtip device that weakens the tip vortex and so **reduces induced drag**. It
  is a drag device, not a lift device.
- **Tricycle gear** — third wheel under the nose. **Conventional gear** (a taildragger) — third
  wheel at the tail.
