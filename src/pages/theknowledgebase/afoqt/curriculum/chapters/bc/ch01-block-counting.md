# Block Counting — the method

**30 questions. 4.5 minutes. 9.0 seconds each.** That is the tightest clock on the entire
AFOQT — Table Reading gives you 10.5, Word Knowledge 12. Nothing else comes close.

It feeds **CSO** and **ABM**. It carries no knowledge whatsoever: there is nothing to forget on
test day, nothing that goes stale, no formula to misremember. Like Table Reading, this is pure
trainable speed, and practice converts to score here faster than almost anywhere else.

The Air Force describes the task as being able to *"see into"* a three-dimensional pile of
blocks. You are given a numbered block and you count how many other blocks it touches.

---

## Rule 1 — faces touch. Corners do not.

This is the whole subtest, stated once, officially:

> **"Blocks are considered touching only if all or part of their faces touch. Blocks that only
> touch corners do not count. All of the blocks in each pile are the same size and shape."**

Read the middle sentence again, because it is the one that costs points. In a stacked pile,
plenty of blocks *appear* adjacent — they sit diagonally up-and-over from your block, their
outlines meet on the page, and your eye files them as neighbours. They are not. If the only
thing they share is an edge or a corner, they do not count.

The Air Force works this out loud on its own sample. Block S3:

> *"S3 touches the faces of the two blocks above it, the two blocks below it, and the blocks
> directly to the right and left of it. **It does not touch the faces of the two blocks
> diagonally below it to the right or left. These blocks only touch the corners of S3 and do
> not count.** Therefore, S3 touches six blocks."*

**Six, not eight.** Two of the eight blocks crowded around it were corner contacts. That is a
25% error on one question, and it is the single most common way to lose this subtest.

> **The test for a real touch:** could you slide a sheet of paper flat between them and have it
> lie against *both*? If they meet only along a line or at a point, no.

---

## Rule 2 — the answer key moves every single question

Look at the official key for five consecutive sample questions:

| Question | A | B | C | D | E |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S1 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| S2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| S3 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 |
| S4 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
| S5 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |

The options are always five consecutive numbers, but **the window slides**. On S1 the answer 3
is **C**. On S2 the answer 3 would be **A**. On S3 it is not on the page at all.

> **You cannot memorise "4 is usually C". There is no usually.**

And it gets worse — published answer keys also run **descending**, so a question can offer
`A6 B5 C4 D3 E2`. Under a nine-second clock the temptation is to count, glance, and grab the
letter you expected. Build the opposite habit now:

**Count first. Commit to the number. Then read the options and find it.**

Never let the option list influence the count. Drills here deliberately shuffle the window and
sometimes reverse it, so that habit is the only one that survives.

---

## The scan order — always the same three questions

Nine seconds is not enough time to invent an approach per pile. Use one order, every time:

1. **Above** — what rests on it?
2. **Below** — what is it resting on?
3. **Alongside** — what is flush against its sides and ends?

Say the running total out loud in your head as you go: *"two above, four, five."* A fixed order
means you never double-count and never re-scan, which is where the seconds actually go.

### Then: what can't you see?

The blocks are stacked in solid layers — the pile has no hidden hollows. So if your block sits
on a lower course, **something is underneath it even when the drawing gives you no line for it.**
That is precisely the *"see into the pile"* the directions are talking about, and it is the
difference between a 4 and a 5 on a lot of items.

The two mistakes are mirror images, and the drills name them when you make one:

- **Counted corners** — you included a diagonal neighbour. Too high.
- **Missed a hidden block** — you counted only what was drawn. Too low.

---

## Pace

At 9.0 seconds per question you have time to count and nothing else. Most correct answers land
in the **3 to 6** range, so a total of 9 should make you re-check — but only *once*.

The Air Force's own instructions say plainly that many people do not finish. **There is no
penalty for a wrong answer**, so a blank is strictly worse than a guess. If a block is buried in
a way you cannot resolve, pick the middle option and move — the clock costs you more than the
question does.
