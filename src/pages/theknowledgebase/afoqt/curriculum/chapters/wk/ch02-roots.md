# Latin and Greek roots

Word Knowledge draws from a genuinely unbounded vocabulary - 25 questions, and no two
candidates who sit the real test necessarily see the same headword. Memorizing individual words
scales badly against that. Roots do not: a Latin or Greek root shows up inside dozens of English
words at once, so recognizing one gives you a foothold on words you have never specifically
studied.

This is not a hunch. The official AF Word Knowledge syllabus lists exactly two study modules,
and one of them is "Parts of a Word (prefix, root, suffix)" - the worked solutions on real OATTS
items reach for it at the step where the headword itself is unfamiliar. Chapter 3 covers
prefixes and suffixes; this chapter covers the roots they attach to.

---

## 1. Latin roots

Most of English's Latin layer arrived through French and reads as the more formal register - the
longer word you would reach for in writing rather than speech. Learn the root and every word
built on it opens up at once.

| Root | Meaning | You already know it from |
|---|---|---|
| `port-` | to carry | im**port**, ex**port**, **port**able |
| `dict-` | to say | pre**dict**, **dict**ionary, ver**dict** |
| `spec-` | to look, to watch | **spec**tator, in**spec**t, **spec**tacle |
| `aud-` | to hear | **aud**ience, **aud**ible, **aud**itorium |
| `scrib-` | to write | de**scrib**e, tran**scrib**e, **scrib**ble |
| `mit-` | to send | trans**mit**, per**mit**, ad**mit** |
| `manu-` | hand | **manu**al, **manu**facture, **manu**script |

Once you can see `port-` inside "portable," the pattern generalizes for free: anything built on
`port-` involves carrying, whether that is goods across a border (import, export) or an idea
across a page.

## 2. Greek roots

Greek roots skew scientific and technical - they built the vocabulary of medicine, media and
measurement, and they combine with EACH OTHER more freely than Latin roots do.

| Root | Meaning | You already know it from |
|---|---|---|
| `photo-` | light | **photo**graph, **photo**synthesis |
| `tele-` | far off, distant | **tele**vision, **tele**phone, **tele**scope |
| `phon-` | sound | tele**phon**e, **phon**ics, micro**phon**e |
| `chron-` | time | **chron**ic, syn**chron**ize, **chron**ology |
| `anthrop-` | human being | **anthrop**ology, mis**anthrop**e |
| `derm-` | skin | **derm**atologist, epi**derm**is |
| `bio-` | life | **bio**logy, anti**bio**tic |

`tele-` plus `phon-` is the whole reason "telephone" means what it means: a device for sound
(`phon-`) at a distance (`tele-`). That is the payoff - two roots you know separately, combined,
decode a word you have never seen defined.

---

## 3. When two roots share a topic - direction still matters

A root gives you a topic, not automatically a direction. `bene-` and `mal-` are a genuine pair:
both are about character or outcome, and they point opposite ways.

- `bene-` - good, well: **bene**volent, **bene**fit, **bene**diction
- `mal-` - bad, badly: **mal**icious, **mal**function, **mal**nourished

A candidate who half-remembers "something to do with how something turns out" and picks blind
between them is exactly as likely to be wrong as right. The fix is the one Chapter 1 teaches for
whole words, applied to a word part instead: know which side of the line you are on, not just
which line.

---

## Before you move on

| Situation | Do this |
|---|---|
| The whole word is unfamiliar | Look for a root you recognize inside it and start from what THAT means |
| You can pull one Latin root out of an unfamiliar word | Expect a formal-register meaning - writing, reporting, carrying, sending |
| You can pull one Greek root out of an unfamiliar word | Expect a technical or scientific meaning, and check whether a second root is riding along |
| Two roots feel related but you are not sure which way | Confirm the DIRECTION (bene-/mal-, good/bad) before you commit - a topic match is not a meaning match |
