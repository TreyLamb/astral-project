# Communication

Four judgment tests about how information moves between people - delivering it, receiving it,
disagreeing about it, and passing it up the chain.

---

## 1. Tactful feedback - deliver a hard message privately and specifically

The test: does the response correct or address an issue in a way that fixes the actual problem
without unnecessary embarrassment - specific, private, and focused on the behavior rather than
delivered publicly or avoided entirely?

> A coworker has been formatting a recurring report incorrectly for two weeks. The best response
> is a brief, private, specific correction - not raising it in front of the team, and not silently
> fixing it yourself every time, which means they never actually learn the correct format.

**The trap:** silently working around someone's mistake feels considerate, but it denies them the
chance to actually learn, and the same error keeps recurring.

---

## 2. Receiving feedback - seek clarity and commit to a change, don't defend or deflect

The test: when criticism arrives, does the response seek the specific example or detail behind
it and commit to something concrete - rather than explaining it away, minimizing it, or silently
absorbing it without engaging?

> A supervisor says your writing has come across as curt, feedback you find genuinely surprising.
> The best response asks for a specific example so you can actually evaluate the pattern - not
> disagreeing and ending the conversation, and not accepting it silently without knowing exactly
> what to change.

**The trap:** even a polite response - apologizing repeatedly, agreeing right away - fails this
test if it never actually asks what specifically to do differently.

---

## 3. Respectful dissent - raise a genuine concern directly, privately, and with your reasoning

The test: does the response voice a real, substantive disagreement to the person who made the
decision, through direct and reasoned explanation - rather than staying silent, being
insubordinate, or working around the decision quietly instead of raising it?

> You believe a new procedure your supervisor just announced has a specific flaw based on direct
> past experience. The best response requests a brief private conversation to explain the concern,
> then supports whatever they decide - not silence because the decision is already made, and not
> objecting loudly in front of the team.

**The trap:** quietly complying while working around the parts you disagree with means the actual
concern never reaches anyone who could act on it - it just creates an invisible gap between
policy and practice.

---

## 4. Proper escalation - route a concern to the person who can act on it, honestly framed

The test: does the response bring an observation or suspicion to the person actually positioned
to address it - honestly described as what it is (a suspicion, an observation), not overstated
as certainty and not acted on directly without the authority to do so?

> You have a strong suspicion about the cause of a recurring process problem but no direct
> authority to change it. The best response brings the observation, clearly labeled as a
> suspicion, to the person who owns the process - not changing it yourself, and not overstating
> the suspicion as a confirmed fact to make sure it's taken seriously.

**The trap:** overstating your certainty to get attention risks the wrong response entirely -
sitting on a real observation because you're not 100% sure is just as much a failure to escalate.

---

## Before you move on

| Signal | Do this |
|---|---|
| You notice a coworker's mistake | Correct it privately and specifically - don't ignore it, fix it silently for them, or embarrass them publicly |
| You receive criticism, especially if it surprises you | Ask for the specific example behind it before agreeing or disputing |
| You disagree with a decision that's already been announced | Raise it directly and privately with your reasoning, then support the final call |
| You have an observation you're not fully certain about | Escalate it honestly labeled as a suspicion - don't overstate it, and don't sit on it |
