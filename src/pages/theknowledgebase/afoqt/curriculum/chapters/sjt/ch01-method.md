# How the subtest actually works

Situational Judgment is unlike every other subtest on the AFOQT, in a way worth understanding
before you see your first item. This chapter has no scenarios of its own - the two skills it
teaches are exercised by every single question in every chapter that follows, the same way
Verbal Analogies' "the two formats" chapter underlies every analogy question without a dedicated
quiz of its own.

---

## 1. Two questions, one situation, back to back

Every item presents a short scenario - a paragraph describing a situation an actual junior
officer (O1-O3) might face - followed by five possible actions, labeled A through E. You answer
**two questions about the same five actions**:

1. Which action is the **MOST effective** response?
2. Which action is the **LEAST effective** response?

The situation and its five actions stay the same for both questions. You are not choosing "the
right answer" once - you are ranking the full field, picking the best and the worst, in that
order, before moving to a new situation.

---

## 2. There is no fixed answer key - you are scored against consensus

This is the genuinely distinctive part, and it changes how you should think about every item.
Every other AFOQT subtest has one objectively correct answer, derivable from a formula or a fact.
Situational Judgment does not. Your response is scored **relative to the consensus judgment of a
wide sample of experienced U.S. Air Force officers** who were shown the same scenario.

That means the right frame for a candidate is not *"what is the textbook-correct answer here?"* -
there is no textbook. The right frame is: **"what would a well-regarded, experienced officer
actually do here?"** Think about the response that reflects sound, professional judgment applied
by someone who has been in situations like this before - not the most cautious answer, not the
most aggressive one, and not what feels personally natural to you if it would not hold up to an
experienced officer's judgment.

---

## 3. No guessing penalty - always answer both questions

Exactly like every other AFOQT subtest, there is no penalty for a wrong answer beyond not
earning the point. Never leave either the MOST or the LEAST question blank. If you are genuinely
unsure, eliminate the options that are clearly better or clearly worse than the rest, and make
your best judgment call on what remains - a guess still has a chance of matching consensus; a
blank never does.

---

## 4. Five actions, one already-correct set of options

Because a situation always has exactly five actions and you are asked to identify the best and
the worst among them, there is no shortfall to manage the way there sometimes is on other
subtests - the field of options never needs padding, and every option deserves a real read. A
common mistake is skimming past the four "obviously fine" options to compare only two you first
noticed - the actual best or worst answer is sometimes among the ones that looked unremarkable on
a first pass.

---

## Before you move on

| Signal | Do this |
|---|---|
| You see a scenario followed by five lettered actions | Expect two questions about the same five: MOST effective, then LEAST effective |
| You're tempted to look for "the textbook answer" | There isn't one - ask what an experienced, well-regarded officer would actually do |
| You're unsure and tempted to skip a question | Never skip - there is no guessing penalty, so eliminate the clear extremes and commit to your best judgment on what's left |
| You've picked an answer after reading only two of the five options | Read all five - the real best or worst is sometimes the one that looked unremarkable at first |
