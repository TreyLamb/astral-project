# Resource management

Three judgment tests about handling limited people, equipment, and time well.

---

## 1. Prioritization under scarcity - rank by actual consequence, not by order or convenience

The test: when several genuine needs compete for one limited resource, does the response rank
them by real urgency and real consequence - not by who asked first, who is senior, or splitting
the resource evenly regardless of need?

> Three team members each have a legitimate reason to need the same piece of equipment this
> afternoon: one has a hard external deadline, one has a flexible internal one, one simply
> prefers to go early. The best response assigns it in that order of actual urgency - not by who
> asked first, and not by dividing the afternoon into equal blocks regardless of need.

**The trap:** treating every request as equally urgent - by splitting time evenly, or by
first-come-first-served - is itself a prioritization failure when the actual stakes clearly differ.

---

## 2. Proper channels for requests - route it through the correct chain, not around it

The test: does the response use the actual process for a resource request, even when a personal
connection or a direct approach offers a shortcut - and escalate through that process if there's
a real timing problem, rather than working around it?

> You could get equipment faster by asking a personal contact directly, off the books, instead of
> the standard ten-day request process. The best response uses the standard process, and asks
> about an expedited option if the timeline is a genuine problem - not the personal shortcut,
> which skips the accountability the process exists to provide.

**The trap:** even *using the process while also quietly working the personal connection in
parallel* still routes part of the request around the official channel.

---

## 3. Realistic commitment - say what's actually achievable, and name the real tradeoff

The test: does the response give an honest, specific picture of what can genuinely be delivered
and what it would cost - neither over-promising and hiding the cost, nor flatly refusing without
explaining what would actually need to change?

> A supervisor asks if a project can be delivered two weeks early. Hitting that date would
> require real overtime or cutting a testing step. The best response says so plainly and lays out
> the specific tradeoff - not a silent agreement that quietly cuts corners, and not a bare "no"
> that leaves the supervisor with nothing to work with.

**The trap:** agreeing to an unrealistic date while quietly planning to cut corners defers the
failure to a later, less controlled moment - it does not actually solve anything.

---

## Before you move on

| Signal | Do this |
|---|---|
| Several genuine needs compete for one limited resource | Rank by actual urgency and consequence - not by who asked first or splitting evenly |
| A personal connection offers a faster route around the standard process | Use the standard process anyway, and escalate through it if timing is a real problem |
| You're asked to commit to something you're not sure is achievable | State honestly what's realistic and name the specific tradeoff - don't over-promise or flatly refuse |
