Verbal Analogies asks one question in two different shapes. The second one is nearly three
times as common as the first in real items — which is exactly why every drill in this app is
built in that shape.
This is not a test of word knowledg. As you can tell by all of the examples below, none of these words are 'difficult' words. This is a test of relations among the words. How do the words relate to each other?


# Part to whole, member to category, part to part, and sequence

This chapter covers the single biggest cluster of real items - about three in ten. Four
relations, and all four answer some version of "how are these two things arranged relative to
each other," as opposed to what one does to the other (chapter 3) or what one means relative to
the other (chapter 4). Because they resemble each other at a glance, this is also where the
level-of-association check from chapter 1 earns its keep the most.

---

## 1. Part to whole

**a is a physical or functional PART of b, and b is the specific whole a belongs to.**

> FINGER is to HAND. A finger is not a hand - it is one part attached to a hand.
> PISTON is to ENGINE. A piston is a moving part inside an engine, not an engine on its own.
> KEEL is to SHIP. The keel is the structural spine along the bottom of a ship's hull.

**The level trap:** the whole has to be the SPECIFIC thing the part belongs to, not a level too
far up. A finger belongs to a hand, not to "a person" - that skips a level. ROOM is to BUILDING
works; ROOM is to CITY does not (too many intermediate levels between a room and a city).

---

## 2. Member to category

**a genuinely IS a b - one member of the broader category b names.**

> ROBIN is to BIRD. A robin genuinely is a bird - a member of that whole category.
> TROUT is to FISH. A trout is a fish.
> OAK is to TREE. An oak is a tree - a specific kind, not a piece of one.

**The category-level trap** runs the other direction from part-whole's: the category has to be
the IMMEDIATE one, not a level too broad. ROBIN is to BIRD works; ROBIN is to ANIMAL does not
(too broad - a robin is *also* an animal, but that's not the tightest true category).

**The quick test that tells part-whole and member-category apart:** try saying "a IS A b."
"A finger IS a hand" is false (finger is a PART of a hand) - part-whole. "A robin IS a bird" is
true - member-category. Run this test before committing to either relation.

---

## 3. Part to part

**a and b are CO-EQUAL parts of the SAME whole - neither one contains the other.**

> MERCURY is to VENUS. Co-equal planets of the same solar system - siblings, not one containing
> the other.
> STARBOARD is to PORT. The two co-equal sides of the same ship.

**The swap test:** could you exchange a and b and still have a valid pair? For part-part, yes -
VENUS is to MERCURY works exactly as well as MERCURY is to VENUS. That is what makes this
relation different from part-whole, where swapping breaks it (HAND is to FINGER does not read the
same way FINGER is to HAND does).

**Where this gets confused with part-whole:** INFANTRY is to CAVALRY (part-part - two co-equal
combat arms of the same army) can look like PLATOON is to ARMY (part-whole - a platoon is a
substructure inside the army's chain of command) at a glance, because both pairs are military and
both put a smaller military noun next to a larger one. The swap test separates them: cavalry and
infantry can trade places in the sentence; a platoon and an army cannot.

---

## 4. Sequence

**a precedes b in a fixed, ordered PROCESS - the order is inherent, not just incidental.**

> EGG is to CHICK. An egg hatches into a chick - the egg always comes first in that process.
> BLUEPRINT is to BUILDING. A blueprint is drawn before the building it describes is ever built.

**The inherent-order test:** the order has to come from the process itself, not from an outside
convention. SEED is to SEEDLING passes (growth genuinely runs one direction). MONDAY is to
TUESDAY would fail this test even though it's ordered - the order is calendar convention, not a
process one thing turns into the other through.

---

## Before you move on

| You're looking at... | Ask this |
|---|---|
| Two nouns, one seems smaller/contained | "Is a a PART of b, or a MEMBER of category b?" - try "a IS A b" |
| Two same-scale nouns from a shared whole | Could you swap them and the sentence still work? If yes, part-part |
| Two nouns where one clearly happens before the other | Is the order built into the process itself, or just convention? |
| A pair that "looks military" or "looks biological" | Don't stop at the domain - run the level-of-association check from chapter 1 |

# Cause to effect, and doer to action

Two relations that both describe what happens BETWEEN two things, but running in different
directions - one is about what one thing makes happen, the other is about what defines what
something does.

---

## 1. Cause to effect

**a directly causes b. The effect has to be IMMEDIATE - one step, not a chain of downstream
consequences.**

> FIRE is to SMOKE. Fire directly produces smoke - one step, nothing in between.
> FRICTION is to HEAT. Friction directly generates heat.
> LIGHTNING is to THUNDER. Lightning directly produces the thunder that follows it.

**The chain-length trap:** FIRE is to SMOKE passes. FIRE is to HOMELESSNESS does not - there are
too many intermediate steps between a fire and someone losing their home (the fire has to spread,
destroy a structure, displace a person...). A cause-effect pair should feel like ONE step, not a
short story.

**Order matters here.** This relation is never symmetric - SMOKE is to FIRE reverses the base
pair and is a genuine wrong answer, not a technicality. Smoke does not cause fire.

---

## 2. Doer to action

**a is the DEFINING action of b - the thing b is known for, not merely something b sometimes
happens to do.**

> BARK is to DOG. Barking is what a dog is known for - the defining action.
> DIAGNOSE is to PHYSICIAN. Diagnosing illness is the defining action of a physician - it is the
> job itself.

**The defining-vs-incidental test:** ask whether the action is what b IS known for, or just
something b occasionally does. BARK is to DOG passes. EAT is to DOG fails - dogs eat, but so does
nearly everything, so eating does not define what a dog is.

**Watch the parts of speech:** a is usually a verb and b is usually a noun (BARK, a verb; DOG, a
noun), though the relation itself is what matters, not the word class - stay with whichever part
of speech the actual pair uses.

---

## The error mode that blurs the two together

A cause-effect pair whose effect happens to also read like something an agent DOES can look like
a doer-to-action pair at a glance. INFECTION is to FEVER (cause-effect: an infection triggers a
fever) sits close to DIAGNOSE is to PHYSICIAN (doer-to-action: diagnosing defines a physician) on
the page - both are medical, both put a process-sounding word next to a medical noun. The
separating question: does a MAKE b happen (cause-effect), or does a name what b characteristically
DOES (doer-to-action)? An infection does not "do" a fever the way a physician "does" a diagnosis.

---

## Before you move on

| You're looking at... | Ask this |
|---|---|
| a and b are both things/events, not a person doing something | Does a directly MAKE b happen - in one step, not a chain? |
| a is a verb, b names a person or role | Is a the thing b is KNOWN for, or just something b occasionally does? |
| An option repeats the base pair's own two words, swapped | Cause-effect and doer-to-action are never symmetric - a reversed pair is a real wrong answer |
| A medical- or profession-flavored pair that could read either way | Ask "does a MAKE b happen" versus "does a name what b DOES" |


# Synonym, antonym and degree

Three relations about meaning itself, rather than about how two things are arranged (chapter 2)
or what one does to the other (chapter 3). The whole chapter comes down to one test you can run
on any pair in about a second.

---

## The one test: "is a a weaker (or stronger) form of b?"

> - **Yes -> DEGREE.** WARM is a weaker form of HOT. Same dimension (temperature), different
>   intensity.
> - **No -> SYNONYM.** HAPPY is not a weaker form of GLAD - they are equally happy. Same
>   meaning, no intensity difference at all.

Run this test before deciding between synonym and degree. It is easy to see two words that both
"mean angry" and assume synonym without checking whether one is simply the stronger version of
the same feeling.

> ANNOYED is to ANGRY - annoyed is a weaker form of angry. Degree.
> HAPPY is to GLAD - neither is a stronger version of the other. Synonym.

---

## 1. Synonym

**a and b share the same core meaning. Order never matters.**

> FAST is to QUICK. BEGIN is to START. TERSE is to BRIEF.

Because swapping the two words changes nothing about the relation, the reversed-pair planted wrong answer
(the base pair's own words in the wrong order) is never used against a synonym pair - GLAD is to
HAPPY would be just as correct as HAPPY is to GLAD, so offering it as a "wrong" option would
secretly be a second right answer. You will not see that trap here the way you do elsewhere.

---

## 2. Antonym

**Direct opposites. Also order-independent, for the same reason as synonym.**

> HOT is to COLD. UP is to DOWN. BENEVOLENT is to MALEVOLENT.

**The natural-direction trap:** a pair only counts as a clean antonym if the OPPOSITE reads
naturally in either direction. The natural opposite of LOVE is HATE - not INDIFFERENCE, even
though indifference is also "not love." If you have to think about which word is the "more
natural" opposite, it probably is not a clean antonym pair.

---

## 3. Degree

**a and b share the same underlying dimension, at different intensities. Order MATTERS.**

> WARM is to HOT (temperature). TRICKLE is to FLOOD (water flow). IRRITATED is to FURIOUS
> (anger).

Unlike synonym and antonym, degree is never symmetric - HOT is to WARM reverses the direction of
increase, and that is a real, meaningful error, not a technicality. If the base pair is WARM is
to HOT (weak to strong), an option that flips it to HOT is to WARM is testing whether you noticed
which direction the intensity runs.

---

## Before you move on

| You're looking at... | Ask this |
|---|---|
| Two words that seem to "mean the same thing" | Can you say "a is a weaker/stronger form of b"? If yes, it's degree, not synonym |
| Two words that seem opposite | Does the reverse pairing feel just as natural? If not, it may not be a clean antonym |
| An option offers the base pair's own words, reversed | For synonym/antonym: not a real trap - order never mattered. For degree: it IS a real trap - direction matters |

# What defines it

The smallest chapter in this track - one relation, two readings of it.

---

## The classic reading: a is the defining quality of b

> FIERCE is to TIGER. Fierceness is what a tiger is known for - a defining quality, not an
> incidental fact.
> SLY is to FOX. Slyness is the defining quality of a fox.

**The definitional-vs-incidental test:** is this quality part of what b IS, or just a fact that
happens to be true about b? FIERCE is to TIGER passes. STRIPED is to TIGER would fail - stripes
are a true fact about tigers, but they are not the defining thing about a tiger the way ferocity
is.

---

## The worker-domain reading: a's role is defined by setting b

> PILOT is to COCKPIT. The cockpit defines a pilot's role - it is where the skill that makes them
> a pilot is exercised, not just a place they happen to be.
> SURGEON is to HOSPITAL. A surgeon in a grocery store is still a surgeon, but the hospital is
> where that role is defined.

This is the same underlying question as the classic reading - "does b define a, or just describe
an incidental fact about a?" - applied to a role instead of a quality. **The same test applies:**
does the setting define a's role, or merely locate them? A surgeon operating outside a hospital
(in an emergency, say) is still recognizably a surgeon; the hospital is what the ROLE is
organized around, which is what makes the pairing definitional rather than incidental.

---

## Why these two count as one relation, not two

Both readings answer the exact same question - "what defines this?" - just applied to a quality
in one case and a role in the other. On the real test you will see both inside the same set of
answer choices, and a question built on the classic reading can be answered correctly by a pair
built on the worker-domain reading, because they share the identical underlying relation. Do not
treat "this one's about a job" as a signal that it must be a different kind of question.

---

## Before you move on

| You're looking at... | Ask this |
|---|---|
| An adjective (or a quality-noun) paired with a person or animal | Is this quality part of what b IS, or just a true-but-incidental fact about b? |
| A role or occupation paired with a place or subject-matter | Does that setting define the role, or would b still clearly be a-the-role without it? |
| A pair that "feels like a job description" versus one that "feels like a trait" | Both are the same relation here - do not treat them as competing answers |
