# Part to whole, member to category, part to part, and sequence

This chapter covers the single biggest cluster of real items - about three in ten. Four
relations, and all four answer some version of "how are these two things arranged relative to
each other," as opposed to what one does to the other (chapter 3) or what one means relative to
the other (chapter 4). Because they resemble each other at a glance, this is also where the
level-of-association check from chapter 1 earns its keep the most.

---

## 1. Part to whole

**a is a physical or functional PART of b, and b is the specific whole a belongs to.**

> FINGER is to HAND. A finger is not a hand - it is one part attached to a hand.
> PISTON is to ENGINE. A piston is a moving part inside an engine, not an engine on its own.
> KEEL is to SHIP. The keel is the structural spine along the bottom of a ship's hull.

**The level trap:** the whole has to be the SPECIFIC thing the part belongs to, not a level too
far up. A finger belongs to a hand, not to "a person" - that skips a level. ROOM is to BUILDING
works; ROOM is to CITY does not (too many intermediate levels between a room and a city).

---

## 2. Member to category

**a genuinely IS a b - one member of the broader category b names.**

> ROBIN is to BIRD. A robin genuinely is a bird - a member of that whole category.
> TROUT is to FISH. A trout is a fish.
> OAK is to TREE. An oak is a tree - a specific kind, not a piece of one.

**The category-level trap** runs the other direction from part-whole's: the category has to be
the IMMEDIATE one, not a level too broad. ROBIN is to BIRD works; ROBIN is to ANIMAL does not
(too broad - a robin is *also* an animal, but that's not the tightest true category).

**The quick test that tells part-whole and member-category apart:** try saying "a IS A b."
"A finger IS a hand" is false (finger is a PART of a hand) - part-whole. "A robin IS a bird" is
true - member-category. Run this test before committing to either relation.

---

## 3. Part to part

**a and b are CO-EQUAL parts of the SAME whole - neither one contains the other.**

> MERCURY is to VENUS. Co-equal planets of the same solar system - siblings, not one containing
> the other.
> STARBOARD is to PORT. The two co-equal sides of the same ship.

**The swap test:** could you exchange a and b and still have a valid pair? For part-part, yes -
VENUS is to MERCURY works exactly as well as MERCURY is to VENUS. That is what makes this
relation different from part-whole, where swapping breaks it (HAND is to FINGER does not read the
same way FINGER is to HAND does).

**Where this gets confused with part-whole:** INFANTRY is to CAVALRY (part-part - two co-equal
combat arms of the same army) can look like PLATOON is to ARMY (part-whole - a platoon is a
substructure inside the army's chain of command) at a glance, because both pairs are military and
both put a smaller military noun next to a larger one. The swap test separates them: cavalry and
infantry can trade places in the sentence; a platoon and an army cannot.

---

## 4. Sequence

**a precedes b in a fixed, ordered PROCESS - the order is inherent, not just incidental.**

> EGG is to CHICK. An egg hatches into a chick - the egg always comes first in that process.
> BLUEPRINT is to BUILDING. A blueprint is drawn before the building it describes is ever built.

**The inherent-order test:** the order has to come from the process itself, not from an outside
convention. SEED is to SEEDLING passes (growth genuinely runs one direction). MONDAY is to
TUESDAY would fail this test even though it's ordered - the order is calendar convention, not a
process one thing turns into the other through.

---

## Before you move on

| You're looking at... | Ask this |
|---|---|
| Two nouns, one seems smaller/contained | "Is a a PART of b, or a MEMBER of category b?" - try "a IS A b" |
| Two same-scale nouns from a shared whole | Could you swap them and the sentence still work? If yes, part-part |
| Two nouns where one clearly happens before the other | Is the order built into the process itself, or just convention? |
| A pair that "looks military" or "looks biological" | Don't stop at the domain - run the level-of-association check from chapter 1 |
