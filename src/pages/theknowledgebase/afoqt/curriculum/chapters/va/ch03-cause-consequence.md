# Cause to effect, and doer to action

Two relations that both describe what happens BETWEEN two things, but running in different
directions - one is about what one thing makes happen, the other is about what defines what
something does.

---

## 1. Cause to effect

**a directly causes b. The effect has to be IMMEDIATE - one step, not a chain of downstream
consequences.**

> FIRE is to SMOKE. Fire directly produces smoke - one step, nothing in between.
> FRICTION is to HEAT. Friction directly generates heat.
> LIGHTNING is to THUNDER. Lightning directly produces the thunder that follows it.

**The chain-length trap:** FIRE is to SMOKE passes. FIRE is to HOMELESSNESS does not - there are
too many intermediate steps between a fire and someone losing their home (the fire has to spread,
destroy a structure, displace a person...). A cause-effect pair should feel like ONE step, not a
short story.

**Order matters here.** This relation is never symmetric - SMOKE is to FIRE reverses the base
pair and is a genuine wrong answer, not a technicality. Smoke does not cause fire.

---

## 2. Doer to action

**a is the DEFINING action of b - the thing b is known for, not merely something b sometimes
happens to do.**

> BARK is to DOG. Barking is what a dog is known for - the defining action.
> DIAGNOSE is to PHYSICIAN. Diagnosing illness is the defining action of a physician - it is the
> job itself.

**The defining-vs-incidental test:** ask whether the action is what b IS known for, or just
something b occasionally does. BARK is to DOG passes. EAT is to DOG fails - dogs eat, but so does
nearly everything, so eating does not define what a dog is.

**Watch the parts of speech:** a is usually a verb and b is usually a noun (BARK, a verb; DOG, a
noun), though the relation itself is what matters, not the word class - stay with whichever part
of speech the actual pair uses.

---

## The error mode that blurs the two together

A cause-effect pair whose effect happens to also read like something an agent DOES can look like
a doer-to-action pair at a glance. INFECTION is to FEVER (cause-effect: an infection triggers a
fever) sits close to DIAGNOSE is to PHYSICIAN (doer-to-action: diagnosing defines a physician) on
the page - both are medical, both put a process-sounding word next to a medical noun. The
separating question: does a MAKE b happen (cause-effect), or does a name what b characteristically
DOES (doer-to-action)? An infection does not "do" a fever the way a physician "does" a diagnosis.

---

## Before you move on

| You're looking at... | Ask this |
|---|---|
| a and b are both things/events, not a person doing something | Does a directly MAKE b happen - in one step, not a chain? |
| a is a verb, b names a person or role | Is a the thing b is KNOWN for, or just something b occasionally does? |
| An option repeats the base pair's own two words, swapped | Cause-effect and doer-to-action are never symmetric - a reversed pair is a real wrong answer |
| A medical- or profession-flavored pair that could read either way | Ask "does a MAKE b happen" versus "does a name what b DOES" |
