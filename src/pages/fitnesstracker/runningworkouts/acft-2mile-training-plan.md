---

## Read this first: the honest assessment

A 6-minute drop over 2 miles in ~5 months is aggressive. It is **not impossible** — you're rebuilding, not starting from zero, and a body with a real aerobic history tends to come back faster than a true beginner's — but it requires everything to go right: consistent training, no injury setbacks, and real progressive overload instead of guessing week to week. That last part is exactly what the previous plan skipped, and it's the single biggest lever available to you.

Two things worth sitting with:
- **Checkpoints exist so you know early if the timeline needs to flex**, not so you can beat yourself up if you miss one. If you're behind pace at Checkpoint 2, we adjust the back half rather than panic.
- **Your heart rate (180–185 after the first mile) at what you're calling "maintainable" pace is high for that effort level.** That could just mean your aerobic base is currently low (very fixable — that's what Phase 1 is for), or it could mean your easy pace has been running too hot, or it could be a personal HR quirk. It's worth knowing your resting HR and, if you haven't had one recently, a basic checkup — not because anything sounds wrong, but because you're about to ramp training load and it's good info to have. This isn't medical advice, just a "cheap to check, expensive to ignore" flag.

---

## The core problem with "just add speed work"

Your gap to close isn't primarily a speed problem — a 10:00/mile jogger and a 7:00/mile runner aren't separated mainly by top-end speed, they're separated by **aerobic capacity, running economy, and the ability to sustain hard effort**. That's why this plan spends its first 40% of duration on aerobic base and durability before threshold work, and doesn't touch real race-pace specificity until the final phase. Front-loading speed work now, before your engine can support it, is how people plateau or get hurt.

## The 3 phases

### Phase 1 — Rebuild Durability (Cycles 1–5, ~Weeks 1–8)
**Goal:** run consistently without digging a fatigue hole. Build long run from 2mi toward 4.5mi. No formal threshold or hill work yet — strides only.

### Phase 2 — Aerobic Strength & Threshold (Cycles 6–10, ~Weeks 9–16)
**Goal:** make "comfortably hard" pace feel normal. Formal threshold sessions begin. Hills reintroduced *very* gradually here at the earliest, given your ankle history — never sustained hill running, only short controlled hill sprints once your base is solid.

### Phase 3 — 2-Mile Specificity & Taper (Cycles 11–14, ~Weeks 17–22)
**Goal:** train the actual demands of the test. Threshold work is replaced by race-pace and faster-than-race-pace repeats (400s/800s). Volume comes down as intensity peaks. Final cycle tapers into test day.

Built around your constraints: one true rest day, back-focused lifting kept separate from leg-heavy days, squats paired with recovery time rather than stacked right after your speed day, and room for hike/ruck.

**Hike/ruck progression:** start ~15–20 lb, add ~5 lb every 2–4 weeks only if the previous weight felt fully manageable. Keep this on the easy Day 9 slot, never the day before or after a quality run.

## Progressive overload — the part that got missed last time

This is the actual mechanism for improvement: load increases in planned, measured steps, with a deload every 3rd cycle so your body absorbs the work instead of just accumulating fatigue. Deload cycles double as your checkpoint test weeks (test when relatively fresh, not exhausted).

**How to set the actual pace each cycle:** after every checkpoint test, recalculate your threshold/interval paces off that new number (roughly: threshold effort ≈ current 2-mile pace + 45–60 sec/mile; race-pace reps ≈ your *goal* pace for that checkpoint stage, from the reference chart above). Don't run intervals off your original 20:00 baseline once you've moved past it — that's the adaptive piece a static plan can't do.

## Hills: the honest answer

Hill work builds leg power, mechanics, and running economy — genuinely useful, but not mandatory for a flat 2-mile test the way it might be for a hilly race. Given your ankle history, the risk/reward doesn't favor rushing it. This plan holds off any hill work until Phase 2 at the earliest (Cycle 6+), and even then only short, controlled sprints on a gentle grade with full recovery — never sustained hill running or long climbs. If your ankle flares at all, drop hills entirely and substitute flat sprints; you lose little by skipping them.

## Guardrails

- **Ankle:** any sharp pain (not just normal muscle fatigue) is a stop sign for that session, not a push-through sign.
- **If a deload/checkpoint week shows no improvement or you feel worse than the previous checkpoint**, that's the signal to extend Phase 1 or 2 rather than force the calendar — better to hit 14:30–15:00 healthy than blow up chasing 14:00 and get hurt at week 12.
- **Two hard days should never sit back-to-back** — the template above already spaces them, but if life reshuffles your week, protect that spacing over anything else.
- This is a training framework, not medical or physical-therapy guidance — if the ankle or anything else flares up meaningfully, that's worth a professional look, not a workaround from me.
