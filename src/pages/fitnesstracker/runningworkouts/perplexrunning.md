Absolutely — here’s a beginner-friendly but thorough guide to running training, inspired by Steve Magness-style principles like building an aerobic base, using threshold work intelligently, and adding speed without turning every workout into a race. The core idea is to train different systems on purpose, not just “run hard,” because workout design is about the stimulus, the recovery, and the adaptation you want.

Big picture of training
A good running plan usually includes easy runs, long runs, threshold work, and some faster sessions, with each one serving a different purpose. Magness emphasizes that workouts should be designed around the whole context — pace, volume, rest, and season — rather than copied as one fixed template.

Think of training like building layers:

Easy running builds the base and helps you recover.

Threshold work teaches you to hold a strong effort for longer.

Speed work improves mechanics, power, and top-end ability.

Race-specific work connects everything to your goal event.

The main workout types
Easy runs
Easy runs are comfortable aerobic runs where you can breathe normally and talk in full sentences. They should feel controlled, not like a workout you need to “win.”

These runs matter because they build fitness without creating too much fatigue, which lets you train more consistently. A lot of runners make the mistake of running easy days too hard, which steals energy from the important sessions.

Long runs
Long runs are simply your longer aerobic runs, usually done at an easy pace. They build endurance, mental toughness, and the ability to stay efficient when you are tired.

For beginners, the long run does not need to be extreme. It should usually be long relative to your normal runs, but still controlled enough that you could finish and feel like you had more left.

Threshold runs
Threshold work is sustained running at a hard-but-controlled effort, often described as the pace you can hold for a while without blowing up. It sits near the point where lactate starts accumulating faster than your body can clear it, so training there improves your ability to sustain speed.

Magness-style threshold training often starts a little below true threshold, then gradually increases time at that effort. Beginners may start with about 10 minutes total at threshold effort and build toward 20 to 25 minutes, while more advanced runners may build longer.

Interval workouts
Intervals are repeated hard efforts with recovery between them. The goal can be aerobic development, threshold development, speed, or race-specific fitness, depending on the length and intensity of the repeats and how much rest you take.

A workout like 5 x 1,000 meters with short rest is very different from 8 x 400 meters with long rest. The rep distance, pace, and recovery together determine the training effect.

Speed work
Speed work means short, fast running done with plenty of recovery so your form stays relaxed and sharp. In Magness’s approach, speed often starts with submaximal work like strides and short hill sprints before progressing to faster sprinting or speed endurance.

This kind of training is not about suffering. It is about staying technically good while moving fast, which helps improve coordination, efficiency, and power.

What lactate threshold means
Lactate threshold is the effort level where your body starts producing lactate faster than it can clear it, so the workout feels “comfortably hard” at first and then becomes difficult to sustain if you push too far. In practical terms, threshold is often around the effort you could hold for roughly 30 to 60 minutes, depending on the runner and context.

For beginners, the best way to think about it is:

Easy = conversational.

Threshold = short sentences only.

Hard interval/speed = very limited talking.

Magness’s recent lactate-threshold guidance emphasizes accumulating time just below threshold rather than living right on the edge, and using short rests so the aerobic system stays engaged.

How to do a threshold run
A simple threshold run can be done as one continuous block or as intervals. The key is that the effort should be strong but controlled — you should not be sprinting or gasping early.

Option 1: Continuous tempo
Warm up 10 to 15 minutes easy.

Run 15 to 30 minutes at threshold effort.

Cool down 10 minutes easy.

Option 2: Broken threshold
Warm up.

Do 3 x 10 minutes at threshold with 1 to 2 minutes easy jogging.

Cool down.

Option 3: Shorter threshold reps
Warm up.

Do 5 x 5 minutes at threshold with short recoveries.

Cool down.

How it should feel:

Breathing is hard but controlled.

You can say a few words, not full conversation.

You finish tired, but not wrecked.

You could probably do one more rep if forced, but not many.

How to do speed training correctly
Speed training works best when you are fresh enough to run with good mechanics. That means you need a thorough warm-up, full or near-full recovery, and a volume that stays small enough to preserve quality.

A common beginner mistake is turning speed day into a conditioning workout. True speed work should let each rep be fast, clean, and relaxed, with enough rest that the next rep is still technically good.

Good beginner speed sessions
Strides: 4 to 8 short fast accelerations after an easy run.

Hill sprints: 4 to 8 reps of 6 to 8 seconds uphill with full recovery.

Short sprints: 4 to 6 reps of 60 to 100 meters with full recovery.

What good speed looks like
Your form stays smooth.

You are not tying up or straining.

You can repeat the effort without major slowing.

You recover enough to keep the next rep high quality.

Steve Magness specifically recommends starting with submaximal speed, like strides and short hills, before progressing to faster flat sprints and speed endurance.

Common workout terms
Here are the most useful running terms in plain English:

Aerobic: Using oxygen efficiently for longer efforts.

Anaerobic: Higher-intensity work where energy demand rises faster than oxygen-based systems can fully cover it.

Threshold: The “comfortably hard” line where you are working hard but still controlled.

Tempo: Often used loosely, but usually means a sustained hard aerobic effort, often near threshold.

Interval: Repeated work bouts with rest between them.

Rep: One single repeat inside a workout.

Strides: Short, relaxed accelerations, usually 15 to 25 seconds, done to improve mechanics and speed without fatigue.

Hill sprints: Very short uphill accelerations with full recovery, used to improve power and form.

Recovery: Easy jogging, walking, or rest between reps.

Easy pace: Conversational effort.

Race pace: The pace you expect to run in a race.

Mileage: Total running distance in a week.

How to build a weekly plan
A simple beginner week might look like this:

Day	Workout	Purpose
Monday	Easy run	Recovery and aerobic base
Tuesday	Threshold or intervals	Fitness development
Wednesday	Easy run	Recover and build volume
Thursday	Strides or hill sprints	Speed and mechanics
Friday	Easy run or rest	Recovery
Saturday	Long run	Endurance
Sunday	Easy run or rest	Recovery
The main rule is to keep the easy days truly easy so the hard days can actually be hard. Magness’s philosophy strongly supports balancing training stress instead of making every day moderate-hard.

How to design a workout guide
If you want to make your own workout guide, organize each workout by purpose, effort, and recovery. That way you know not just what to do, but why you are doing it.

Use a format like this:

Workout name.

Goal.

Warm-up.

Main set.

Recovery.

Cool-down.

How it should feel.

Common mistakes.

Example:

Workout name: Threshold intervals.

Goal: Improve ability to hold hard effort.

Warm-up: 10 to 15 minutes easy + drills.

Main set: 3 x 10 minutes at threshold with 2 minutes jog.

Cool-down: 10 minutes easy.

Feel: Hard but controlled.

Mistakes: Going too fast, resting too long, or turning it into a race.

Mistakes to avoid
The most common running mistakes are going too hard on easy days, racing workouts, and using speed work when tired enough that form falls apart. These errors usually make training feel impressive but reduce the actual benefit.

Another common mistake is copying advanced workouts too early. Magness’s material repeatedly points toward gradual progression, where you start with simple submaximal sessions and build volume or intensity over time.

Starter progression
If you are new to structured running, start simple:

Build easy mileage first.

Add strides once or twice per week.

Add one threshold workout per week.

Add hills or short speed after that.

Only later add more advanced intervals or race-specific work.

That sequence keeps the training focused on adaptation instead of just exhaustion. It also makes the workouts easier to understand and recover from.

Using this in practice
A practical beginner threshold day could be: 10 minutes easy warm-up, 3 x 8 minutes at threshold with 2 minutes jog, then 10 minutes cool-down. A practical beginner speed day could be: easy run, drills, 4 to 6 strides, and maybe 4 x 8-second hill sprints with full recovery.

Those two sessions already cover a lot of ground: one builds sustained fitness, the other improves mechanics and sharpness. Done consistently, they are far more useful than random hard running.
