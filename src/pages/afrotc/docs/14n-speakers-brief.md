## Part 2. Your three questions, answered

### Question 1. What is an SEI?

SEI stands for **Special Experience Identifier**. It is a tracking code in your personnel record.

- DAFMAN 36-2100 says SEIs "identify special experience and training not otherwise identified." They help assignment officers find someone who already has a certain experience. They "are not substitutes for AFSCs." [3]
- An officer SEI is a three-character code. The first character is an **activity code**. The last two are an **experience set**, which names a system, a level of experience, or a type of experience. [1]
- A unit commander awards the SEI once you have met the qualification criteria for that experience set. [1]
- Once awarded, it stays in your record and does not expire. It can be withdrawn if you lose the qualification, for example if you lose your clearance or become medically disqualified. [1] [3]
- The officer directory says there are "no AFSC constraints." Any AFSC can hold any SEI. [1]

- An officer SEI is not even tied to a specialty. DAFMAN 36-2100 says the personnel system "has no capability to associate officer SEIs with AFSCs." You earn one by meeting its training and experience criteria, not just by sitting in a coded position. [3]

**What SEIs exist for 14Ns?** The current tables are on a login-only site (myFSS), so I could not open them. The only published list I could open is the 2010 officer directory. Its experience sets "for use with AFSCs 14NX" are: Targeting (TM), Analysis (TN), Space (TO), Support to Ground Operations (TP), Force Management (TQ), ISR Operations (TS), Geospatial (TU), Resource Management (TV), Special Operations (TW), Air-to-Air (TX), Air-to-Ground (TZ), Tactical Air Control System (T1), Low Observable (T2), Strategic Mobility Operations (T3), Imagery Intelligence (T5), HUMINT (T6), Collection Management (T7), SIGINT (T8), MASINT (T9), Systems Management (T0), Cryptologic Career Program (TT), and Intelligence Resource Management (TY). It lists nothing for special access programs. [22] That list is 16 years old, so say "I am working from the 2010 directory" if asked. I found no current Guard vacancy notice that names a specific 14N SEI.

**Short version you can say:** "SEIs are tags on your record for special experience, such as targeting, collection management, HUMINT, or SIGINT. You stay a 14N. The tag helps the assignment system find you for jobs that need that experience."

### Question 2. If a 14N earns an SEI, are they still a 14N?

**Yes.** An SEI does not change your AFSC. Your AFSC is still 14N1, 14N3, or 14N4, depending on your skill level. The SEI is an extra line on your record. The directory's own words are that SEIs are "not substitutes for AFSCs." [3]

### Question 3. Are the five INTs performed by enlisted Airmen, with officers only fusing the results? Or do some officers specialize too?

The sources support a split that is close to your picture, but not an exact one.

**What enlisted Airmen do.** Most enlisted intelligence specialties are organized around a discipline [2]:

- 1N1X1 Geospatial Intelligence
- 1N2X1 Signals Intelligence
- 1N3X1 Cryptologic Language Analyst
- 1N4X1 Cyber Intelligence, and 1N4X2 Cryptologic Analyst and Reporter
- 1N7X1 Human Intelligence Specialist
- 1N0X1 All Source Intelligence Analyst
- 1N8X1 Targeting Analyst

The directory describes each of these as Airmen who "perform" the work and, at higher levels, "manage" it. There is no enlisted specialty titled MASINT or OSINT. [2]

**What officers do.** There is one officer specialty, 14N. It has no discipline-specific versions. The directory says officers "utilize subject matter expertise in the six intelligence disciplines" while they "lead Airmen through the intelligence process" across four areas: Analysis, Collection, Targeting, and Sensing Grid Activities. [1]

So officers are expected to **know** all six disciplines, **lead** the Airmen who work them, and **fuse** the results. But the directory also gives officers hands-on duties: producing "fused intelligence analysis" and "directing, scheduling, and controlling specific collection platforms, sensors and HUMINT sources." So "officers only fuse" is too narrow. [1]

**Why there is one officer specialty.** A 2009 RAND study says nine older officer specialties were merged into a single 14N in 1994 to produce "broadened specialists." Officers it interviewed wanted broad knowledge plus "specific training in one of the areas of expertise." [26]

**Can an officer specialize?** Yes, through assignments and SEIs. For example, an officer who works as a targeteer for years can earn a targeting SEI. The one published list of 14N experience sets is the 2010 directory. [22]

**One-sentence answer you can say:** "Enlisted Airmen mostly work one discipline each. An intelligence officer is trained across all of them, leads those Airmen, and fuses what they produce for the commander. Officers can build depth in one area through assignments."

### Slide 3. Where intelligence fits

**At the unit.** At a flying wing, the intelligence flight normally sits in the Operations Support Squadron under the Operations Group. The staff designator for intelligence is **A2** in the Air Force, **J2** on a joint staff, **G2** for the Army, **S2** at a battalion or brigade, and **N2** for the Navy. [18] [38]

**MAJCOMs.** ACC's fact sheet calls it the lead command for fighter, command and control, intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance. MAJCOM intelligence staffs oversee training, qualification, and standards. The Air Force intelligence instruction that sets this is AFMAN 14-402 (27 January 2025). I saw that only as a search snippet. [19]

**16th Air Force (Air Forces Cyber).** It was activated 11 October 2019 at Joint Base San Antonio-Lackland. It took in the former 24th and 25th Air Forces. Its fact sheet says it is responsible for the Air Force ISR mission, weather forecasting for the joint force, cryptologic activities, and network and cyber operations. It reports about 45,000 Total Force Airmen at 123 locations. [39]

**480th ISR Wing.** Headquartered at Joint Base Langley-Eustis, Virginia. It is the lead wing for the Air Force Distributed Common Ground System (AF DCGS, the AN/GSQ-272 SENTINEL weapon system). Its fact sheet says over 6,000 Airmen. [36] Do not quote "$5B+ in resources." I found no source. The "20 terabytes a day" figure is from a 2014 Defense One article, so say "about 20 terabytes a day as of 2014," or leave it out. The motto "Non Potestis Latere" ("You Can't Hide") appears in a 2013 ClearanceJobs article but I found it on no official page. Say "reported motto" if you use it. [36]

**Reachback.** The idea that a lot of Air Force intelligence work is done from home station, while the sensor is half a world away, is **not sourced** in what I checked. The fact sheets confirm that the 480th is the lead wing for DCGS. I did not find a source that uses the word "reachback" or says how much work is done from home station, so treat the word as our shorthand. [36]

**National level.** NASIC is at Wright-Patterson AFB, Ohio. Its mission is to "discover and characterize air, space, missile, and cyber threats." It also exploits captured foreign equipment. Its heritage goes back to 1917 at McCook Field. [20] NSIC is the Space Force's counterpart, also at Wright-Patterson. [21] DIA provides military intelligence to warfighters and defense policymakers. NSA leads U.S. signals intelligence and cybersecurity. NGA's director is the national functional manager for geospatial intelligence. [21]

**Intelligence and cyber at headquarters.** In August 2024 DefenseScoop reported that the Air Force would split its combined A2/6 directorate, so that "the A2 will go back to focus solely on intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance matters." In July 2025 it reported that the Air Force "broke the 6 function away from the 2 on the Air Staff" and created a new A6 for warfighter communications and cyber systems. Under the 2024 plan, electromagnetic spectrum, cyber, and information operations moved to the A3. I could not confirm that those moves happened exactly as planned, or the A2's exact current title, and NASIC's fact sheet still names the older combined title. Do not call the new A6 "cyber effects." **How many get commissioned each year.** AFROTC is the largest source, about 2,000 second lieutenants a year (2,109 into the Air Force in 2022). OTS graduated over 2,200 officers in fiscal 2023, counting all components. The Academy's Class of 2026 sent 805 to the Air Force and 94 to the Space Force. OTS peaked at 7,894 in 1967. Fact sheets for AFROTC and OTS were seen as snippets only. [46]

### Slide 5. The training pipeline

**Course length.** Not officially published anywhere I could reach. See Part 1 and the slide notes.

**Skill levels.** 14N1 on completing the initial skills course. 14N3 after mission qualification and at least 36 months performing intelligence functions. 14N4 for staff positions. There is no 14N2 level. An older 2002 training plan says 18 months for 14N3. The current directory says 36. [1]

**Goodfellow.** The 17th Training Group says it trains all of the Air Force's intelligence courses. Some training happens elsewhere: language training at the Defense Language Institute in Monterey, human intelligence at Fort Huachuca, and cyber and signals courses at Corry Station. The International Intelligence Training Center at Goodfellow trains students from more than 80 partner nations. [8]

**The 315th Training Squadron.** More than 3,300 students a year and over 220 permanent staff. That is the population the 14N course sits inside. It is not the number of new intelligence officers. [8]

### Slide 6. Why intel exists

**The official description of the 14N.** From the officer directory: "Leads and performs intelligence activities across the full range of military operations supporting the Air Force's Service Core Function (SCF) of Global Integrated Intelligence, Surveillance and Reconnaissance (ISR)." [1]

**Data, information, intelligence.** JP 2-0 draws this line in its Figure I-1. Data from a sensor becomes information once it is processed into a form people can understand. Information related to other information and to experience "may be termed intelligence." The new Air Force doctrine puts it this way: intelligence "is the product of analyzing information to understand its significance, predict future actions, and identify vulnerabilities." [9] [4]

**The intelligence process.** The two descriptions are:

- **Joint doctrine (JP 2-0)** describes six categories of intelligence operations: planning and direction; collection; processing and exploitation; analysis and production; dissemination and integration; and evaluation and feedback. [9]
- **Air Force doctrine.** The newest doctrine, AFDP 2-0 (1 May 2026), adopts the six-step process and calls it **PCPADE**. The older five-letter **PCPAD** is still in the 14N job description in the officer directory. [4] [1]

If someone asks: "Joint doctrine has six steps. The Air Force now calls it PCPADE. You will still see PCPAD in the older job description."

**Decision advantage.** AFDP 2-0 defines it as "the ability to make better, faster, and more effective decisions than an adversary." The Air Force ISR strategic vision from about 2013 is titled "AF ISR 2023: Delivering Decision Advantage." I could not confirm that the current National Intelligence Strategy uses the phrase, so credit it to Air Force doctrine. [4] [6]

**Boyd and OODA.** See Part 1 and the slide notes. Boyd was an Air Force fighter pilot who retired as a colonel in 1975. The OODA loop is in his briefing "Patterns of Conflict." [15]

### Slide 9. The four areas a 14N builds depth in

The four areas are Analysis, Collection, Targeting, and Sensing Grid Activities, from the officer directory. The directory defines them as follows [1]:

- **Analysis.** "Produces timely and accurate fused intelligence analysis" using structured analytic techniques, to "satisfy the commander's priority intelligence requirements and requests for information."
- **Collection.** "Executes collection management by converting intelligence requirements into collection requirements, establishing priorities, tasking or coordinating with appropriate collection sources or agencies, monitoring results, and re-tasking." It also covers "directing, scheduling, and controlling specific collection platforms, sensors and HUMINT sources."
- **Targeting.** "Kinetic and non-kinetic target development, weaponeering, precision point mensuration, force application, execution planning, and combat assessment." Weaponeering means choosing which weapon and how many will achieve the effect.
- **Sensing Grid Activities.** Collection operations (sensing), analysis (identify and attribute), delivering information to customers, and collaborating with partners (sharing), using cloud technology, machine intelligence, and big data analytics. Older directories list "Integration" in this slot.

**Why targeting is called the most operationally direct.** That wording was ours. What the sources say: targeting covers selecting and prioritizing targets and matching actions to them, and the directory includes "combat assessment." The enlisted targeting training plan lists collateral damage estimation among the duties, alongside target development, weaponeering, and combat assessment. I did not find a source that says targeting "carries the heaviest responsibility," or that ties collateral damage estimation to the Law of Armed Conflict. Those are our judgments. [1] [52]

**HUMINT in the Air Force.** Small. There is an enlisted HUMINT specialty (1N7X1, created in 2015) and officers can earn a HUMINT SEI. A 2015 Air Force Times article put Air Force HUMINT at roughly 40 to 50 Airmen. Army doctrine says the Air Force and Navy "have limited HUMINT collection capability." I found no official source for the claim that the Army "carries most" DoD HUMINT. [14] [47]

**Special Access Programs.** Defined in Executive Order 13526 as programs that impose "safeguarding and access requirements that exceed those normally required for information at the same classification level." Access requires a formal indoctrination. A polygraph may be required. It is a security arrangement, not an intelligence discipline. [30]

### Slide 10. All-source analysis

The real JP 2-0 and DoD Dictionary definition is: "Intelligence products and/or organizations and activities that incorporate all sources of information in the production of finished intelligence." A second sense covers collection: all systems are considered and "those most capable are tasked." [9] [10]

**Estimative language.** ICD 203 requires one of two fixed sets of likelihood words. The first set is: almost no chance (1–5%), very unlikely (5–20%), unlikely (20–45%), roughly even chance (45–55%), likely (55–80%), very likely (80–95%), and almost certain (95–99%). Confidence is stated separately. ICD 203 says a product must not combine a confidence level and a likelihood in the same sentence. [11]

**Source grading.** The scale is A to F for reliability and 1 to 6 for information. "B" is "usually reliable" and "2" is "probably true." F and 6 mean "cannot be judged." This is printed in Appendix B of Army FM 2-22.3 (2006). The names "Admiralty System" and "NATO" come only from secondary sources. [14]

**Structured analytic techniques.** The U.S. Government's *Tradecraft Primer* (March 2009) lists the key assumptions check, Analysis of Competing Hypotheses, and red team analysis. ACH is Richards Heuer's technique, from Chapter 8 of *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis*. It asks the analyst to look for evidence that disconfirms each hypothesis instead of evidence that supports a favorite. [31]

**Mirror imaging, and when intelligence is wrong.** The warning against assuming an adversary will think as you do is a standard point in analytic training. This brief does **not** have a source for it, and I did not research the history of specific intelligence failures. If asked about a specific failure, say you have not studied it.

## Part 4. Questions you are likely to be asked

**Do you need a technical degree?**
No. airforce.com lists science, humanities, social sciences, structured analysis, engineering, and mathematics, so most majors qualify. Ask your detachment for the exact degree list. [7]

**What does the N in 14N stand for?**
"It is a classification code. The 1 is the career group, Operations. The 14 is the intelligence and information operations field. The N is the functional-area letter." I found no source that gives the N a meaning. [3]

**Do you run spies?**
No. A 14N is a military staff officer who directs collection and produces analysis for a commander. Counterintelligence inside the Air Force belongs to OSI, not to 14N. The Air Force does have a small HUMINT force. [1] [14]

**Do you deploy?**
The directory does not say. "It depends heavily on the unit" is accurate.

**Can you tell us anything classified?**
"I do not hold a clearance yet, so everything in this brief comes from public sources: Air Force directories, published doctrine, and unit fact sheets."

**How competitive is the career field?**
Do not invent a number. I found no public figure. Say you would ask the detachment for the allocation data.

**What if I cannot get a clearance?**
The specialty requires access to Top Secret and SCI material. The directory says losing your clearance means your 14N AFSC is withdrawn. Disclosing issues is better than hiding them, but that point is general advice and **not sourced**. [1]

**What is the difference between intelligence and cyber?**
Different specialties. The 14N directory excludes computer hardware and software research and communications security, which belong to the cyber field (17-series), and excludes counterintelligence, which belongs to OSI. If the headquarters split comes up, say: "The Air Staff split intelligence from communications and cyber in 2025, and intelligence went back to focusing on ISR." [1] [51]

**How is targeting different from the rest of intel?**
It is one of the four areas every 14N works in. Some people specialize in it, and there is an enlisted targeting career field. [1] [2]

**How long is the training?**
"Several months at Goodfellow, and I would have to check the exact length."

**What is an SEI? Do you stay a 14N?**
See Part 2.

**What does "all-source" mean?**
Use the JP 2-0 definition in Part 3.

**Something you genuinely do not know.**
"I do not know. I would rather say that than guess. I can find out and get back to you."

### Three stories

**Operation Bolo, 2 January 1967.** The National Museum of the U.S. Air Force says Col Robin Olds, commander of the 8th Tactical Fighter Wing, and the wing's tactics officer, Capt John "J.B." Stone, devised the plan. F-4s flew the same route, altitude, and formation as an F-105 strike and carried the F-105s' electronic jamming pods. Despite overcast weather, seven MiG-21s, "about half of their operational force," were shot down in a 12-minute engagement with no U.S. losses. Seven is the U.S. count. The call-sign detail comes from secondary accounts, not the museum. [41]

**The U-2 and Cuba, October 1962.** On 14 October 1962 a U-2 flown by Air Force Major Richard Heyser photographed western Cuba. National Photographic Interpretation Center analysts found three medium-range ballistic missile sites under construction near San Cristobal. The photographs were analyzed on 15 October and President Kennedy was briefed on 16 October. Analysts then estimated when sites would be ready, and revised those estimates in numbered supplements. [42]

**Desert Storm, 1991.** The 38-day air campaign ran from 17 January to 23 February 1991. The ground war began on 24 February. Air operations continued until the ceasefire on 28 February. The claim that this is "the origin of much of the targeting methodology a 14N learns today" is **not sourced**. [43]

### What is changing

**China.** DoD's 2024 China Military Power Report says "PLA texts emphasize using cyber operations and other capabilities to degrade adversary C4ISR, weapon systems, and support nodes early in a conflict to seize information dominance." The FY2026 Air Force posture statement called China the "sole pacing threat." The newest one (April 2026) does not use the word "pacing." It describes the focus as "Deterring China in the Indo-Pacific." Use "pacing" only with the year. [49]

**OSINT.** The 2024 intelligence community OSINT strategy calls it "increasingly important and growing in demand." Reporting names social media and commercial satellite imagery as the drivers. [28]

**AI and machine learning.** The officer directory says sensing grid work uses "cloud technology, machine intelligence, and big data analytics." That is all I can source. The claim that automation is being used for triage, not judgment, is our framing and **not sourced**. [1]

### Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| AFSC | Air Force Specialty Code. The job code. 14N is the officer code. 1N is the enlisted intelligence family. |
| A2 / J2 / G2 / S2 / N2 | Intelligence staff designators: Air Force, joint, Army, battalion or brigade (also Marine Corps), and Navy. |
| ACH | Analysis of Competing Hypotheses. A technique that looks for evidence that disconfirms each explanation. |
| ADSC | Active Duty Service Commitment. Four years for a non-rated AFROTC officer. |
| AFOCD / DAFOCD | The Officer Classification Directory. It defines each officer specialty. |
| AFECD / DAFECD | The Enlisted Classification Directory. |
| CFETP | Career Field Education and Training Plan. |
| COMINT / ELINT / FISINT | The three sub-types of SIGINT: communications, electronic (such as radar), and foreign instrumentation (such as missile telemetry). |
| DCGS | Distributed Common Ground System. The Air Force's networked system for processing and exploiting ISR data. |
| ISR | Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance. |
| MAJCOM | Major Command, such as ACC, AMC, AFSOC, or PACAF. |
| OODA | Observe, Orient, Decide, Act. |
| OSI | Office of Special Investigations. Air Force counterintelligence and criminal investigation. Not 14N. |
| OSS | Operations Support Squadron. Where a wing's intelligence flight normally sits. |
| PCPAD / PCPADE | The intelligence process. Planning and direction, Collection, Processing and exploitation, Analysis and production, Dissemination, and (new) Evaluation and feedback. |
| SCI | Sensitive Compartmented Information. Needs its own access beyond Top Secret. |
| SCIF | Sensitive Compartmented Information Facility. An accredited, access-controlled room. |
| SEI | Special Experience Identifier. A tracking code for extra experience. Not a job. |
| Tier 5 | The federal background investigation for Top Secret and SCI. Formerly the SSBI. |
