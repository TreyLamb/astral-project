00How to use this

Your deck is a 3–5 minute talk. This is the other 95% — the material you draw on when someone asks a follow-up, and the background that makes the delivered version sound like you understand it rather than recite it.

Read it once end to end. Then, before you brief, re-read §01, §14 and §15. Those three carry almost all the practical value on the day.

Professional habit worth starting now

Everything in this brief is open source — official Air Force pages, published joint doctrine, unit fact sheets, and reporting. You do not hold a clearance yet, so you have nothing classified to protect, but the discipline starts before the clearance does: cite where a fact came from, and when you don't know, say you don't know. In this career field, confidently stating something you can't source is the actual failure mode. Practising "I don't know, but here's how I'd find out" in a cadet briefing is not a weakness — it's the habit the job is built on.

01The core idea

If you remember one distinction, make it this one:

Data
A raw reading. A radar return. A line of intercepted traffic. Meaningless alone.
Information
Data that has been processed into something legible — a translated message, a geolocated image.
Intelligence
Information that has been analysed against a decision-maker's actual question. It is defined by the question it answers, not by how it was collected.

That last line is the whole career field. An intelligence officer is not a person who collects secrets; they are a person who converts what is knowable into what is decidable. A perfectly accurate report that reaches a commander after the aircraft has launched is not intelligence. It's history.

Decision advantage

The term your deck uses on slide 4 is the current doctrinal framing, and it's worth being able to unpack it. Decision advantage means making better decisions, faster, than the adversary can react to — which means intelligence is measured relatively, against an opponent's tempo, not absolutely against the truth.

This is where the OODA loop earns its place in the brief. Colonel John Boyd — a USAF fighter pilot — built it from a study of why American F‑86s beat the technically superior MiG‑15 in Korea. His answer was not better hardware; it was a faster decision cycle. Observe, Orient, Decide, Act. Whoever cycles faster forces the opponent to keep responding to a situation that has already changed.

The point most people miss

Intelligence doesn't feed the whole loop — it feeds Orient, the step where a commander's mental model of the situation is built. Boyd considered Orient the decisive step. So bad intelligence doesn't merely slow the loop; it corrupts it, and the commander then acts quickly and confidently on a wrong picture. That is a worse outcome than acting slowly. If anyone asks "why does this job matter," that is the strongest available answer.

02The intelligence cycle

Your deck doesn't cover this and it is the single biggest gap. The cycle is the formal process the whole career field runs on, and it's the most likely thing a cadre member with an intel background will probe.

The Air Force teaches it as PCPAD — five phases, with assessment and feedback running throughout:

  1. Planning & Direction

    A commander states what they need to know. Those become formal intelligence requirements, and the gaps between what's needed and what's known drive everything downstream. This is where an officer spends much of their time.

  2. Collection

    Sensors and sources are tasked against those gaps. Note the verb: collection is tasked, not merely received. Deciding which scarce sensor goes after which gap is its own specialty (collection management).

  3. Processing & Exploitation

    Raw take becomes usable — signals demodulated, imagery rendered, languages translated. Enormous, unglamorous, and where most of the machine-learning investment is going.

  4. Analysis & Production

    The judgement step. Fragments from different disciplines are weighed against each other and turned into an assessment with a stated confidence.

  5. Dissemination & Integration

    Getting it to the person who needs it, in a form they can act on, in time. A brilliant assessment in the wrong format at the wrong classification level is a failed product.

Joint vs. Air Force — a real distinction

Joint doctrine (JP 2-0, Joint Intelligence) describes six categories, not five: it breaks out Evaluation & Feedback as a category in its own right rather than folding it through the others. Both framings are current and correct in their own lane. If you're asked, "The Air Force teaches PCPAD as five phases with feedback continuous; joint doctrine splits feedback out as a sixth" is a precise, defensible answer that shows you've read past the recruiting page.

One more thing doctrine stresses and briefers usually forget: it is a cycle, not a pipeline. The output of analysis is usually a new gap, which becomes a new collection requirement. Practically, the job never has a "done" state — you are always handing your own unanswered questions back to the front of the process.

Why fusion is the actual skill

Each INT has a characteristic blind spot, and they're not the same blind spot — which is the entire reason all-source analysis exists:

  • SIGINT tells you what someone said, which may be a lie, a drill, or deliberate deception fed to a channel they know is monitored.
  • GEOINT tells you what was there at one instant — and imagery of a revetment cannot tell you whether the aircraft in it is mission-capable.
  • HUMINT gives you intent, which no sensor can collect — but it is filtered through a human with motives.
  • MASINT can confirm the identity of a system with high confidence while saying nothing about what it will be used for.
  • OSINT is abundant and fast but is the easiest of all to poison deliberately.

Fusion means using each one to cover another's blind spot. When a HUMINT report and imagery disagree, the disagreement is itself information — it usually means someone is concealing something, and that becomes the next collection requirement.

04Analytic tradecraft

This is the material that most separates someone who has read about the job from someone who understands it. Almost none of it is on your slides and any of it will land well.

Source reliability and information credibility are graded separately

A reliable source can still report something implausible; an unreliable source occasionally reports the truth. Analysts therefore rate the two independently. The best-known framework is the NATO Admiralty System: source reliability A–F, information credibility 1–6. A report graded B2 is "usually reliable source, probably true information." An F6 is "reliability cannot be judged, truth cannot be judged" — which is not the same as false.

Words of estimative probability

Analysts are trained not to write "likely" casually, because the reader will act on it. The intelligence community standardises estimative language so that "almost certainly," "likely," "roughly even chance," "unlikely" and "almost no chance" map to defined probability bands, and so that confidence (how good the underlying evidence is) is stated separately from likelihood (how probable the outcome is).

Why this matters more than it sounds

You can have high confidence in a low-probability judgement — strong evidence that something probably won't happen. Collapsing those two into one word is how briefings mislead commanders without anyone lying. If you can explain that distinction cleanly, you will sound like you've actually studied the field.

Structured analytic techniques

Because analysts are human, the community uses formal methods to attack their own reasoning. Worth naming one or two:

  • Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH) — list every plausible explanation, then look for evidence that disconfirms each rather than evidence that supports your favourite. Designed specifically to defeat confirmation bias.
  • Red teaming / devil's advocacy — someone is formally tasked to argue the opposing case so the assessment has to survive a real attack.
  • Key assumptions check — write down what you are taking for granted, then ask what breaks if each assumption is wrong. Most intelligence failures are assumption failures, not collection failures.

The mirror-imaging trap

The classic analytic error is assuming an adversary will behave as you would, given the same facts — weighing their options with your values, your risk tolerance and your doctrine. It has driven some of the community's most expensive misses. Naming this in a briefing signals real reading.

05What a 14N actually does

Air Force documentation organises the officer role around four competencies — a cleaner structure than "a day in the life," and worth having:

Analysis
Fusing all-source reporting into assessments; owning the accuracy of what the unit publishes.
Collection
Writing requirements and managing which sensors and sources get tasked against which gaps.
Targeting
Target development, weaponeering, force application, execution planning and combat assessment — kinetic and non-kinetic.
Sensing Grid
Employing and integrating the ISR sensor enterprise itself as a system.

The day to day, concretely

This is the part of the deck that is easiest to say vaguely and hardest to say well. Four specifics will carry you further than any amount of "fast-paced, dynamic environment."

1. You work inside a SCIF, and that shapes everything

A SCIF (Sensitive Compartmented Information Facility, pronounced "skiff") is an accredited, access-controlled room built to defeat eavesdropping. Practically, for the person working in one:

  • Your phone does not come in. It goes in a locker outside. You are unreachable for most of the working day.
  • Usually no windows. You can finish a shift with no idea what the weather did.
  • Nothing leaves — no notes, no thumb drives, no photographs of the screen.
  • You cannot talk about your day. Not to your spouse, not to your parents, not to the friend who asks what you do. "I work intel for the wing" is roughly the whole answer you are allowed to give.

That last one is a genuine lifestyle cost and is worth saying out loud in a career-field brief, because nobody mentions it and it is the thing people actually find hard.

2. The clock belongs to the flying schedule

At a flying wing the intel shop has to be manned whenever aircraft are flying. If the squadron flies a night sortie, somebody from intel is there to brief it and to debrief it when it lands. In garrison a standard week is Monday–Friday, but some billets — especially at operations centres and DCGS sites — run 12-hour shifts, including nights and weekends. During an exercise or a surge, everybody's hours go up.

3. A realistic day at a flying wing

Not a schedule you should present as doctrine, but a credible shape of a day — and far more useful to an audience than four abstract bullets:

  • Early. In before the aircrew. Read overnight traffic — what came in from national agencies, the theatre, the other INTs — and work out what changed while you were asleep.
  • Mid-morning. Build and deliver the pre-mission threat brief. You stand in front of aircrew, often with fifteen years more experience than you, and tell them what they are flying into. Credibility here is earned by being right repeatedly; it does not come with the rank.
  • Midday. Production. Your analysts are building the unit's intelligence products; you are directing that work, checking it, and answering the questions the morning threw up.
  • Afternoon. The non-glamorous half of the job — database and target folder maintenance, updating the recurring products, answering taskings from the group or wing staff, and the additional duties every Air Force officer carries regardless of AFSC (writing performance reports, award packages, being the unit's safety or training officer).
  • On landing. Post-mission debrief. Capture what the aircrew actually observed, reconcile it against what you told them, and feed the difference back into collection. This is the step that closes the cycle (§02) and the one most briefers forget.

4. You are doing three jobs at once

New lieutenants are consistently surprised by this ratio:

  • Analyst — the work you joined for.
  • Leader of enlisted Airmen — you supervise experienced 1N analysts almost immediately, some of whom have been doing this longer than you have been an adult. Most lieutenants report this, not the analysis, is the hard part.
  • Staff officer — the paperwork of being an officer, which nobody warns you about and which does not care that you are busy.
It varies enormously by assignment — say so

"A day in the life" is really four different lives, and naming them shows you understand the career field rather than one slide of it:

  • Flying wing (OSS): the rhythm above. Closest to operators, most briefing, most visible.
  • DCGS / production centre: shift work, exploiting live collection, supporting operations happening right now, somewhere else in the world. See §09 on reachback.
  • Staff (MAJCOM, HQ): requirements, programming, policy. Less analysis, more influence.
  • Joint or national agency: working alongside DIA, NSA or NGA and the other services. More of the career field ends up here than people expect — see the numbers in §08.
Deployments — answer this honestly

Yes, 14Ns deploy, and some deploy forward with fighter squadrons, special operations units or joint commands. But because of reachback, a large share of Air Force intelligence is performed from home station regardless of where the fighting is. Tempo depends far more on which unit you land in than on the AFSC itself. "It depends heavily on the assignment" is the accurate answer, not a dodge.

Honest framing worth having ready

If someone asks what the downside is: a great deal of intelligence work is routine — maintaining databases, building the same recurring products, briefing a threat picture that hasn't changed in a week. The dramatic version is real but rare. The officers who do well are the ones who care about being right and about the team, rather than the ones who came for the mystique. That answer is more credible than pretending the job is constant excitement, and cadre will respect it.

The four specialty tracks — unpacking slide 10

These four are not separate entry-level jobs. They are where the career field branches after someone is already qualified — an SEI for officers, a follow-on award for enlisted. The slide states each in four or five words; here is what those words actually mean, because every one of them is the kind of phrase an audience will ask you to expand.

Targeting — "the most operationally direct role"

It means the shortest distance between your product and someone pulling a trigger. Most intelligence produces understanding that shapes a decision later. Targeting produces a product that becomes an action, sometimes within hours.

"Kinetic" is the giveaway word in the speaker notes — it means physical force, as opposed to non-kinetic effects like cyber or electronic warfare. Targeting is the most kinetic of the intelligence specialties.

What a targeteer actually does: identify and verify what a thing is; work out its function within the adversary's system; characterise its physical vulnerabilities; recommend what weapon and how many will achieve the desired effect (weaponeering); estimate what else would be damaged (collateral damage estimation — a legal obligation under the Law of Armed Conflict, not a courtesy); and assess afterwards whether it worked and whether it needs re-attack.

Why it carries the heaviest responsibility

If an all-source analyst misjudges intent, the commander makes a worse plan. If a targeteer misidentifies a building, people die who should not have, and it cannot be undone. That asymmetry — not the drama — is the real reason this track is singled out.

"Targeting is where intel output turns into weapons employment — your analysis feeds the strike decision directly, including the legal assessment of what else gets hit."

Fusion Analyst — "works the same layer as all-source"

Be aware that this is the weakest line on slide 10, and sharpen it or be ready for it.

What it is reaching for: fusion analysis is all-source analysis — the same inputs, the same skill of reconciling several INTs into one picture. The intended distinction is tempo, not discipline:

  • All-source builds the deliberate picture on a planning cycle. There is time to weigh evidence, caveat it, and coordinate the assessment.
  • Fusion does the same work in minutes, during an operation, supporting aircraft that are airborne now.

Same discipline, different clock.

Verify this one before you lean on it

"Fusion Analyst" maps to 1N4X1, which in practice carries a strong digital-network and SIGINT flavour rather than being a pure speed variant of 1N0X1 all-source. The slide's framing is a simplification. Check the current enlisted classification directory before presenting it as a clean tempo distinction.

"Same fusing work as all-source, but on an operational clock — supporting a mission in progress rather than planning the next one."

Collection Management — "which sensor or source"

Two words doing deliberate work. They cover the full span of collection:

  • Sensor — a machine. A satellite, a radar, the camera on an RQ-4, a receiver on an RC-135.
  • Source — a person. A HUMINT contact, a liaison relationship, a debriefed traveller.

So "sensor or source" is shorthand for technical and human collection both.

The job itself: there are always far more questions than collection capacity. Satellites pass overhead only at certain times. Aircraft have fuel limits. Sources have access to some things and not others, and pushing them can get them exposed. The collection manager decides what gets looked at, by what, and when.

The real content of the job is opportunity cost

Tasking an asset against question A means question B goes unanswered today. That is why the speaker notes call it one of the hardest judgement calls in intelligence — the decision is not really "what do we collect," it is "what are we willing not to know."

"It's air traffic control for questions — you've got more things you need to know than you have ways to find out, and somebody has to decide what doesn't get answered."

HUMINT / Special Access — two unrelated things on one card

Know that these are separate concepts. The only thing they share is vetting beyond the baseline, which is why they ended up grouped.

HUMINT is intelligence from people, and it is much broader than spies: debriefing defectors and travellers, lawful interrogation of detainees, liaison with partner-nation services, military attachés at embassies, and exploiting captured documents.

Its unique value is the thing no sensor can do — intent. A satellite sees a tank; it cannot see a decision. Only a person can tell you what the other side means to do next week.

Its weakness is the mirror image. Human sources have motives: they can lie, inflate their value to stay useful, be fed false information, or be working for the other side. That is exactly why reliability and credibility are graded separately (§04). "Source handling" is the tradecraft of recruiting, vetting, meeting and protecting a source — and protecting matters literally, because exposure can get someone killed.

Special Access is not a discipline at all. A Special Access Program (SAP) is a security construct: a programme compartmented above normal TS/SCI, with its own access roster, often its own facility and codename. "Additional vetting" means extra scrutiny, sometimes a polygraph, and a formal read-on to that specific programme. You do not apply for it — you are read on because a job requires it.

Know this before you say it

Air Force 14N involvement in HUMINT is limited. The heavy lifting belongs to DIA's Defense Clandestine Service and to the Army, which owns most tactical HUMINT. If someone asks "do Air Force intelligence officers run agents?" — essentially no, not as a standard career path.

"Two different things on one card: HUMINT is a collection discipline, Special Access is a clearance construct. They're grouped because both need vetting past the baseline."

07Pipeline and clearance

Training

All Air Force intelligence training is centred on Goodfellow AFB, Texas, with the 315th Training Squadron running officer training. The initial course — Intelligence Officer Training / the Intelligence Officer Initial Skills Course — is roughly eight to nine weeks and covers the disciplines, analytic tradecraft, collection management, targeting methodology and Air Force ISR systems.

⚠ Course length — sources genuinely conflict, and your slide 9 states one as fact

Open sources give three different answers for the 14N initial course: roughly 8 weeks, roughly 4 months, and — in at least one rendering of the 315 TRS fact sheet — six and a half months. Your slide 9 currently says "8.5-week Intelligence Officer Training."

I could not resolve it, because the authoritative pages are all on af.mil, which returns 403 to every automated request, including with a normal browser user-agent. Compounding it, the course has been actively redesigned — there was a "Learning Next" beta rebuild of Intelligence Officer Training — so older figures may simply be stale rather than wrong.

Verify before you brief this. Anyone who has been through IOT, or your cadre, can settle it in one question — and a wrong course length is exactly the kind of error someone in the room will have lived through. If you cannot confirm it, say "a few months at Goodfellow" and move on.

Goodfellow is a genuinely joint schoolhouse: it trains intelligence personnel for multiple U.S. services and for international partners, which is a nice detail if the conversation turns to joint operations. The 315th Training Squadron alone trains 3,300+ students a year — see §08.

After the initial course, training branches by assignment — follow-on qualification tailored to the platform or mission you'll support, plus home-station training at your gaining unit. A 14N heading to a fighter wing, an RPA unit, a space unit and a production centre do not receive the same follow-on.

Clearance

What it is
A Tier 5 investigation — the deepest level of federal background investigation, formerly called an SSBI. Required for Top Secret and for SCI access.
What it involves
Roughly the last ten years: every address, employer, school, foreign contact and financial record, verified by record checks and interviews — including an Enhanced Subject Interview with you and interviews with people who know you.
How long
Published figures move. Recent reporting puts Top Secret around 6–12 months for most cases, with TS/SCI — which adds SCI indoctrination and sometimes a polygraph — running longer, commonly quoted as 6–18 months.
After it's granted
Continuous vetting has largely replaced the old fixed reinvestigation cycle — you are monitored on an ongoing basis rather than re-investigated every five years.
Applied to slide 8

The slide used to say 12–24 months. That was a fair figure during the big clearance backlog, but it now reads as out of date — current published processing times are materially faster. It now reads "often under a year, varies by background", and the speaker note carries the detail. The honest driver of variance is foreign contacts, foreign travel, financial history and dual citizenship — not paperwork sitting on a desk.

Two things cadets routinely get wrong and you can correct: the investigation is about honesty and vulnerability to coercion, not about having a spotless life. Disclosed past drug use or debt is a manageable fact; the same thing concealed and then discovered is disqualifying. And you generally begin the process before you have the clearance — you'll do useful work at lower classification while it's pending.

09Where intelligence sits

Your slide 12 gives three tiers. Here is the fuller map, with the names that carry weight.

At the unit

Intelligence flights sit in the Operations Support Squadron under a wing's Operations Group. The staff designator for intelligence at any echelon is A2 in the Air Force (J2 joint, G2 Army). If you hear "the A2," that is the intelligence directorate or its chief.

16th Air Force (Air Forces Cyber)

The Air Force's information-warfare numbered air force, stood up in 2019 by merging the old 24th (cyber) and 25th (ISR) Air Forces. It owns the service's ISR mission and capabilities, its cryptologic activities, operational weather, and the defence of Air Force networks. Headquartered at Lackland AFB, Texas. If someone asks "who owns Air Force intelligence," 16 AF is the answer.

480th ISR Wing and AF DCGS

At Joint Base Langley-Eustis, Virginia. It leads the Air Force Distributed Common Ground System (AF DCGS, the AN/GSQ-272 SENTINEL weapon system) — the globally networked system that processes, exploits and disseminates take from U-2, RQ-4, MQ-9 and other platforms, 24/7. Reported at over 6,000 personnel and more than $5B in resources, handling a reported ~20 terabytes of data per day.

The concept behind it

DCGS is the answer to a real problem: the sensor can be over the Pacific while the analyst exploiting its take sits in Virginia. That's called reachback — and it means a lot of Air Force intelligence is performed by people who are not deployed. Good detail if someone asks "do you deploy?" Sometimes yes; but the ISR enterprise deliberately does a great deal of its work from home station.

National level

NASIC
National Air and Space Intelligence Center, Wright-Patterson AFB. The Air Force's centre for characterising foreign air, space, missile and cyber threats. Its assessments shape acquisition programmes, national policy and treaty verification. It also performs foreign materiel exploitation — reverse-engineering adversary systems.
NSIC
National Space Intelligence Center — the Space Force's analytic counterpart, also at Wright-Patterson.
DIA / NSA / NGA
Defense Intelligence Agency (all-source defence intelligence), National Security Agency (SIGINT), National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency (GEOINT). Air Force personnel serve in all three.
Current — and a genuinely good thing to know

The Air Force has been splitting intelligence and cyber apart at headquarters level. The combined A2/6 directorate — which had intelligence, communications and cyber under one three-star — is being separated so that A2 focuses purely on ISR, with a new A6 three-star owning cyber effects, and electromagnetic spectrum operations moving over to the operations (A3) side. It's part of the "re-optimisation for great power competition" effort. Being able to say "intel and cyber are actually being pulled apart right now, not merged" is current, specific, and very few cadets will know it.

11Three stories worth having

A briefing is remembered for its story, not its bullet list. Any one of these is a strong 30-second insert.

Operation Bolo — 2 January 1967 Best one

North Vietnamese MiG-21s would only engage bomb-laden F-105 Thunderchiefs, and avoided F-4 Phantoms. They identified the F-105s partly by the electronic signature of their ECM pods and relied heavily on ground-controlled intercept radar.

Colonel Robin Olds and Captain John Stone of the 8th Tactical Fighter Wing built a deception around exactly that. They sent F-4s flying the F-105 profile: same routes, same altitudes, same speeds, same radio call signs, same ECM pods. They chose a day with overcast skies so the MiGs would have to trust their radar controllers rather than their eyes.

The MiGs committed to the intercept and found Phantoms. In roughly twelve minutes, seven MiG-21s were destroyed — reportedly about half the operational North Vietnamese MiG-21 force — with no U.S. losses.

Why this is the right story for your brief

Bolo was not won by a better aircraft. It was won because someone understood precisely how the adversary decided what to attack — and then fed that decision process a lie. That is intelligence, targeting and deception fused into an operation, and it is the cleanest possible illustration of your own slide-2 Sun Tzu quote, "All warfare is based on deception." It also lands with an aviation audience.

The U-2 and the Cuban Missile Crisis — October 1962

A U-2 overflight returned imagery of Soviet medium-range ballistic missile sites under construction in Cuba. The imagery gave the President a factual basis and — critically — a timeline, since analysts could estimate how long until the sites were operational. It's the canonical case of GEOINT converting a political suspicion into a decision with a clock on it.

Desert Storm — 1991

A 38-day air campaign systematically dismantled Iraqi air defences, command and control, and logistics before the ground war began. The combination of stealth, precision weapons and ISR is the moment modern air targeting as a discipline becomes visible. It's also the origin of much of the targeting methodology a 14N learns today.

12What's changing right now

China is the pacing challenge

Department of the Air Force posture documents describe China as the pacing threat, with the Indo-Pacific as the focal theatre. For intelligence specifically, one detail matters: PLA writings emphasise degrading an adversary's command-and-control and ISR early in a conflict to seize information dominance. In other words, the ISR enterprise is not just a supporting capability — it is itself an early target. That reframes the career field as something that has to be able to operate while being actively attacked.

The OSINT revolution

Commercial satellite imagery, flight and ship trackers, and social media have moved a large amount of what used to require classified collection into the open. The Russia–Ukraine war is the reference case: troop movements were tracked in near real time from commercial imagery and publicly posted video.

The second-order effect is the interesting one, and it's a good thing to raise: when adversary movements are visible to everyone, the analyst's value shifts away from obtaining information and toward verifying it. Open sources are also the easiest to deliberately poison. Volume went up; the premium on judgement went up with it.

AI and machine learning

The pressure point is processing and exploitation — phase three of the cycle, where the data volume is crushing. Computer-vision models are already used against commercial satellite imagery to flag changes for human review. The realistic framing, and the one to use, is that automation is being applied to triage — deciding what a human should look at — rather than to producing the judgement itself.

A strong closing line if you want one

Every one of these trends increases the amount of available information and none of them reduces uncertainty. That's the durable argument for the career field: the bottleneck was never collection, and automation doesn't remove the requirement for someone accountable for the judgement.

13When intelligence is wrong

Optional for a 3–5 minute talk, but have it ready — it is the most likely hard question, and handling it well is more impressive than any fact in this brief.

The community's most-studied failures were overwhelmingly failures of analysis and assumption, not of collection. The recurring pattern: an existing belief frames incoming data; contradicting evidence is explained away; uncertainty gets compressed out of the language as the assessment moves up the chain; and the final consumer hears more confidence than the analysts actually had.

This is precisely why the tradecraft in §04 exists — ACH, key assumptions checks, red teaming and calibrated estimative language are all institutional responses to specific past failures. They were not invented as academic exercises.

If you're asked directly about a specific failure

Answer at the level of process, not politics. "The post-mortems generally found the collection wasn't the weak link — the analytic assumptions and the way uncertainty was communicated were — and a lot of current tradecraft standards exist as a direct response to that" is accurate, professional, and doesn't drag you into a political argument in a cadet briefing. Don't speculate beyond what you've actually read.

14Q&A bank

Ranked roughly by how likely you are to be asked. The italic line is a delivery you can use close to verbatim.

Do you need a technical degree?

No. Any bachelor's degree qualifies. Analytic writing and reasoning matter more than a specific major; language, regional studies, STEM and international relations all show up.

"Any degree qualifies — they train you from zero. What they're actually selecting for is whether you can reason carefully and write clearly under time pressure."

What does the "N" in 14N stand for?

Nothing. AFSC letters are assigned career-field identifiers, not abbreviations. 14-series is officer operations support; 1N is the enlisted intelligence family.

Is it like the movies / do you run spies?

No. Clandestine human source operations are principally a CIA and defence HUMINT mission. A 14N is a military staff officer who directs collection assets and produces assessments for a commander. Counterintelligence inside the Air Force belongs to OSI, not to 14N.

"It's much closer to being an analyst and a manager than a field operative — my job would be making sure the commander's picture of the threat is right, not going out and getting it myself."

Do you deploy?

Yes, but less predictably than many career fields, because of reachback — the DCGS enterprise means a great deal of ISR exploitation happens from home station regardless of where the sensor is. It depends heavily on the unit you're assigned to.

Can you tell us anything classified?

Use this one — it's a free win.

"I don't hold a clearance yet, so everything in this brief is open-source: Air Force career pages, published joint doctrine and unit fact sheets. Honestly, that's most of the job anyway — knowing what's actually knowable from what you have."

How competitive is the career field?

Don't invent a number. 14N is consistently among the more sought-after non-rated fields, and allocations vary year to year with Air Force requirements. Say that, and say you'd check the current AFROTC allocation data rather than guess.

What if I can't get a clearance?

The AFSC requires U.S. citizenship and Top Secret eligibility, so clearance eligibility is a genuine gate. The main risk factors are undisclosed issues, significant foreign contacts or financial problems — and disclosure is almost always better than concealment.

What's the difference between intelligence and cyber?

Good question to get, because of §09. Intelligence produces understanding; cyber operations produce effects. They were organisationally combined at Air Force headquarters under A2/6 and are now deliberately being pulled apart, with A2 focused on ISR and a new A6 on cyber effects.

How does intel differ from targeting?

Targeting is one of the four 14N competencies rather than a separate field. It's the most operationally direct of them — target development, weaponeering and combat assessment — and its product can feed a strike decision, which is why it carries the heaviest responsibility.

What's the hardest part of the job?

"Standing in front of aircrew who've been flying for fifteen years and telling them what they're about to fly into — when you're a brand-new lieutenant. You don't get credibility with your rank; you earn it by being right repeatedly."

Something you genuinely don't know

"I don't know — and I'd rather say that than guess. I'll find out and get back to you."

In this field specifically, that answer is a demonstration of competence, not an admission of failure. Use it without hesitation.
