RomaU.S. Army Air Service · 1921–1922

The largest semi‑rigid airship in the world, bought from Italy, flown by the United States Army Air Service out of Langley Field — and lost in the worst American airship disaster to that date. It is the one airframe in the dossier that is unambiguously Air Force heritage, and what it caused is still in the regulations and still on the awards list today.

01Does it count as an Air Force airframe? Yes.

Answer this before anything else, because it is the first thing cadre will probe.

The one-sentence answer

The Roma was operated by the United States Army Air Service, which is a direct organisational ancestor of the United States Air Force. The Air Force and the Army were a single service until 18 September 1947; the Air Force officially traces its own lineage back to 1 August 1907. An Air Service aircraft is Air Force heritage by the Air Force's own reckoning.

Six of seven fail for the same reason: lighter‑than‑air was overwhelmingly a Navy mission. The Army ran its own airship program from 1908 until 1937, when the Air Corps shut it down and transferred everything remaining to the Navy. The Roma belongs to that Army program.

A fact that makes the whole topic defensible

The U.S. Army's first powered aircraft was an airship, not an aeroplane. Signal Corps Dirigible No. 1 was accepted in 1908 — before the Army bought a Wright aeroplane. Under 20 mph, about two hours' endurance, tested at Fort Myer and Fort Omaha, scrapped in 1912.

So if a cadet asks why you are briefing a blimp in an Air Force program, the answer is that lighter‑than‑air is where Air Force aviation started.

02The airframe — what a semi-rigid actually is

This is the technical heart of the brief, and it is the thing that makes Roma more interesting than a generic blimp: it is the middle of the three airship architectures, and you can explain all three in thirty seconds.

The three architectures. Roma is the one in the middle — and the rarest.

What the keel buys you

On Roma the rigid keel ran the length of the underside and carried the control cabin and the crew spaces inside it — so there was no separate gondola dangling on wires. It was partially articulated, deliberately, so the hull could flex slightly rather than fight the gasbag.

That is the whole semi‑rigid bargain: more rigidity and a stronger structure than a blimp, far less weight and cost than a Zeppelin. It is also the architecture that failed on 21 February 1922 — see section 04.

Who built it

Designed in Italy by a team led by Umberto Nobile, with Celestino Usuelli, Eugenio Prassone and Colonel Gaetano Crocco; built by Stabilimento Costruzioni Aeronautiche (SCA).

Nobile is worth a sentence, because it raises the stakes: four years after Roma he designed and piloted the semi‑rigid Norge, which in 1926 made the first verified flight over the North Pole with Roald Amundsen aboard. Roma was not a crank's project — it came from the leading semi‑rigid designer in the world.

03Three months in American service

Roma's entire US career lasted 98 days. The sequence matters, because several of the problems that killed her were visible beforehand.

  1. 1921 — purchased from Italy
    The Army Air Service buys the ship for $184,000. The United States is openly shopping for airship capability and buying foreign because nobody at home builds anything this size.
  2. August 1921 — arrives in crates
    Dismantled and shipped. On unpacking, the fabric outer cover is found to have mildewed and weakened. That is the first warning, and it is in the record before she ever flew for the US.
  3. Autumn 1921 — assembled at Langley Field
    Rebuilt by US Army Air Service crews in Virginia.
  4. 15 November 1921 — first American flight
    She flies. The NARA photograph in section 01 is from this period.
  5. Late 1921 — a propeller disintegrates in flight
    It rips through the envelope and slashes open a gas bag. The ship returns safely, but that is warning number two.
  6. December 1921 — re-engined
    The six Italian Ansaldos have been unreliable in cold weather. They come out and six American Liberty L‑12s go in — more power, more weight, a different installation on an airframe designed around something else.
  7. 21 February 1922 — destroyed
    First flight with the new Liberty engines. See the next section.
The judgement call worth making out loud

A mildewed cover, a propeller through the gasbag, and an engine swap the airframe was not designed for — then a test flight with the new engines carrying 45 people. Whatever the precise mechanical cause, that passenger manifest is the decision a modern safety officer would stop at. It is a legitimate point to raise in a brief, and it is the kind of observation that shows you did more than read a spec sheet.

0421 February 1922

Roma left Langley Field at 2:00 PM with 45 people aboard for the first flight on the new Liberty engines. She flew down the Chesapeake Bay shoreline — Buckroe Beach, Fort Monroe, Hampton Roads, Willoughby Spit — toward the Norfolk Navy Base.

At about 1,000 feet, over the Army's Norfolk Quartermaster Depot, the box rudder structure failed. Witnesses on the ground described seeing the entire box rudder slip sideways. The ship became uncontrollable in an instant and flew into the ground.

Just before the bow struck, Roma hit high‑voltage power lines. She was inflated with hydrogen. She burst into flames.

Be precise about the cause — sources differ

You will meet two accounts, and the honest move is to give both:

  • Box rudder failure — the structure carrying the control surfaces let go, and eyewitnesses saw it slip sideways. This is the detailed account.
  • Nose collapse at speed — some summaries say the bow caved in during high‑speed flight.

They are not wholly incompatible — both describe a structural failure of a semi‑rigid hull under load, which is the point that matters. What is not in dispute: the structure failed, she hit power lines, and the hydrogen is what turned a crash into a massacre. If you are asked for a single cause, say “structural failure of the control surfaces; the hydrogen is what made it unsurvivable” and note that accounts differ on the detail.

05What it changed — and what is still here

This is the strongest section of your brief, because the consequences are not historical trivia. Three of them are still live in today's Air Force.

1. It ended hydrogen in the US military — permanently

Roma was the last hydrogen‑inflated airship ever flown by the United States military. Every subsequent US Army and Navy airship used non‑flammable helium. Congress acted on it.

This is why your dossier's other American entries — Akron, Macon, the L‑, K‑ and ZPG‑classes — are all helium ships. They are helium because of this crash. Roma is the hinge the rest of the dossier turns on.

And it is the sharpest possible contrast with entry 03: the United States banned hydrogen in its own airships in 1922 and then refused to export the helium alternative, which is precisely why the Hindenburg was still burning hydrogen fifteen years later.

2. It produced the first Cheney Award — still awarded today

Master Sergeant Harry A. Chapman went into the flames to pull his shipmates out. For that he became the first recipient of the Cheney Award, established in 1927 and presented to him by President Calvin Coolidge.

The detail to close your brief on

The Cheney Award is still an active United States Air Force award, given for “an act of valor, extreme fortitude or self‑sacrifice in a humanitarian interest, performed in connection with aircraft.” It is named for 1st Lt William H. Cheney, killed in a mid‑air collision over Foggia, Italy, in January 1918.

A living Air Force tradition begins at this airframe. That is as direct an answer to “why does this matter to us?” as you are going to get.

3. The names are still on the map

  • Langley Field is now Langley Air Force Base — today part of Joint Base Langley‑Eustis. The old airship site there is still called the “LTA” (lighter‑than‑air) area, and there is a Roma Road named in the ship's memory.
  • Scott Field, where the Army's TC‑class blimps flew, is now Scott AFB.
  • Captain Dale Mabry's name was carried by Dale Mabry Field in Tallahassee, Florida. (Flagged: I confirmed Mabry commanded Roma and died in the crash, but did not independently verify the naming of the field — check before asserting it.)

4. The Army got out of airships entirely

The Army Air Corps kept flying lighter‑than‑air for another fifteen years — the Goodyear RS‑1 semi‑rigid (ordered 1923, the first built in America, scrapped 1930) and a long run of TC‑class patrol blimps. In mid‑1937 the Air Corps shut the program down and transferred everything to the Navy; only TC‑13 and TC‑14 were judged serviceable enough to go.

That is why there are no Air Force airships today, and it is the clean closing line for the brief: the Air Force began in lighter‑than‑air and left it in 1937.

06Questions you will get

Is this even an Air Force airframe?

Yes. Operated by the U.S. Army Air Service, a direct ancestor of the USAF — Army and Air Force were one service until 18 September 1947, and the Air Force traces its lineage to 1 August 1907. The original negative of the Norfolk photograph is stamped “U.S. Army Air Service Photographic School.” It was based at Langley Field — now Langley AFB, where a road is named after it.

What was the service called before the Air Force existed?

In order: Aeronautical Division, Signal Corps (1907) → Aviation Section, Signal Corps (1914) → Air Service (1918) → Air Corps (1926) → Army Air Forces (1941) → U.S. Air Force (18 Sep 1947). “Army Air Forces” is the immediate predecessor; Roma was Air Service.

Why was the Army flying airships at all? Isn't that a Navy thing?

Mostly yes — which is why six of the seven airframes in the main dossier are Navy. But the Army's very first powered aircraft was an airship, Signal Corps Dirigible No. 1 in 1908, before it bought an aeroplane. The Army ran coastal patrol and observation airships until 1937, when the Air Corps closed the program and handed the remaining ships to the Navy.

What's a semi-rigid, and why does it matter here?

Three architectures: non‑rigid (no structure, gas pressure only — a blimp), rigid (full metal frame, holds shape empty — a Zeppelin), and semi‑rigid in between: a rigid keel along the bottom and nothing else. On Roma the keel ran the length of the ship and contained the control cabin and crew spaces, and it was deliberately articulated to flex. It matters because it was a structural failure that killed her.

What actually caused the crash?

A structural failure of the box rudder at about 1,000 ft — witnesses saw the whole assembly slip sideways and the ship became instantly uncontrollable. Some accounts instead describe a nose collapse at speed. Both describe a hull/control structure failing under load. She then struck high‑voltage power lines, and the hydrogen did the rest. 34 of 45 aboard died.

Why hydrogen in 1922, when everyone knew it burned?

Helium was scarce, expensive and barely in production; hydrogen was cheap, plentiful and gives more lift. Roma was also an Italian ship designed around hydrogen and delivered that way. The crash ended the argument immediately: Roma was the last hydrogen airship the US military ever flew.

Did changing the engines cause it?

Not directly, and don't claim it did. But it is a legitimate contributing concern worth raising: 21 February 1922 was the first flight with the new Liberty L‑12s, swapped in that December for the unreliable Italian Ansaldos. A different engine installation on an airframe designed around another one, flown as a test with 45 people aboard — plus a cover already found mildewed on arrival and a propeller that had earlier torn through a gas bag. The pattern is what a safety officer would flag.

What's the legacy? Why should we care?

Three things, all still live:

  • Helium. Every US military airship after Roma used it. Akron, Macon, the K‑class and the ZPGs are helium ships because of this crash.
  • The Cheney Award. MSgt Harry A. Chapman went into the fire to pull men out and became its first recipient, decorated by President Coolidge. It is still awarded by the Air Force today.
  • Langley. Still an Air Force base, still has an “LTA area” and a Roma Road.
How big was it, really?

410 ft long, 82 ft in diameter, 1,193,000 ft³ — the largest semi‑rigid airship in the world when the Army bought it. For scale, that is about seventeen times the gas volume of a British SS Zero patrol blimp, with six engines against one.

Who designed it?

Umberto Nobile led the design team (with Usuelli, Prassone and Crocco) for Stabilimento Costruzioni Aeronautiche. Four years later Nobile designed and piloted the semi‑rigid Norge, which made the first verified flight over the North Pole in 1926 with Roald Amundsen aboard.
