Sea ScoutSS Class

Everything behind entry 01 of the dossier: where the name comes from, why it is a “class” at all, every variant that carried the letters SS, the three aeroplane fuselages that were hung under the envelope, and the anatomy of the ship part by part — built from the 1919 account written by an officer who served in the service.

01Why “Sea Scout”, and why a “class”

The name

Both names are real and both are correct, which is why sources disagree. SS stands for Submarine Scout — that is the original and the operational name, and it is what the Royal Naval Air Service called them. Sea Scout appears alongside it in later and secondary usage. Neither is wrong; if a cadet challenges you, the safe answer is:

Submarine Scout. The job was scouting for submarines — the ship was named after its mission, not after the sea. Both expansions appear in the literature; “Submarine Scout” is the original.

That is worth internalising because it also explains the whole design. This was not a general purpose airship that happened to be used against U‑boats. It was a single‑purpose anti‑submarine sentry, built to the cheapest specification that could keep two men and a wireless set loitering over a shipping lane for eight hours.

Why it is called a class — the precise answer

This is the part most people get vague about, and there is a documented, specific reason. Before 1915 the Royal Navy numbered every airship it owned in one single running sequence, regardless of type — No. 1 was the rigid Mayfly, No. 2 the Willows, and so on up to No. 22. When the Admiralty decided to mass‑produce small anti‑submarine ships, that scheme would have collapsed: dozens of near‑identical cheap blimps would have swamped the list and buried the larger ships among them.

So the Navy broke them out. George Whale, who served in the airship service and wrote the standard 1919 account, puts it plainly:

“Each of these ships was accordingly numbered in its own class, S.S., S.S.P., S.S. Zero, Coastal, C Star and North Sea, from 1 onwards as they were completed.” George Whale, British Airships: Past, Present & Future (1919), p. 89
The answer, in one sentence

“Class” is a numbering system, not just a family resemblance. An SS airship is called SS 1, SS 14, SS 40 — numbered within its own class from 1 upward — instead of taking the next number in the Navy's single airship list. The Navy adopted it in 1915 precisely because it was about to build a great many of them. It is the same logic as a ship class: a shared design, built in quantity, numbered as a group.

The rigids kept the old single sequence, which is why their numbers jump so strangely — No. 1, then R 9, then R 23. Whale's own comment on R 80 is that the authorities called it that “in their wisdom — why, nobody knows.”

02Three weeks from order to flight

The origin story is unusually well documented and it is the single best thing you can tell a room, because the dates are absurd.

On 28 February 1915, with U‑boats sinking merchant shipping faster than the Navy could respond, Admiral Fisher sent for Commander E. A. D. Masterman and Wing Commander N. F. Usborne and told them he wanted small, fairly fast airships to operate against submarines — and that he wanted them at once.

The team at RNAS Kingsnorth, on the Hoo Peninsula, did not design anything new. They took a B.E.2c aeroplane, removed its wings, tailfin and elevators, and slung the fuselage underneath a disused envelope from HMA No. 2, the Willows No. 4. That improvised machine became SS 1.

Why this matters for a brief

The SS class is a case study in accepting a worse aircraft sooner. Nobody thought a wingless B.E.2c under a gasbag was elegant. It was available in three weeks, it cost about £2,500, and it could be built by people who already knew how to build aeroplanes. That trade — good enough, now, in quantity — is the entire reason the type existed, and it is a point of doctrine you can defend in front of cadre.

03Yes — there are multiple versions

This is the other half of your question, and the answer is emphatically yes. SS is a family, not a single aircraft. Roughly 158 ships of all SS versions and variants were built, of which only about 60 were the original three car‑types. The rest were successive redesigns that kept the SS letters.

The split matters, because it works on two levels at once:

  • Within the original SS there were three versions, distinguished only by whose aeroplane fuselage was used as the car — B.E.2c, Maurice Farman and Armstrong Whitworth. Same envelope concept, same mission, three different cars.
  • Beyond the original SS came four derivative classes — SSP, SSZ, SST and SSE — each with a purpose‑built car rather than a converted aeroplane body.

Every change in the family was driven by the car, not the gasbag. The envelope went from 60,000 to 70,000 ft³ once and then sat still for the whole war. What kept changing was the thing underneath: borrowed aeroplane body → purpose‑built box → boat hull → twin‑engine boat hull. Endurance more than doubled, 7–8 hours to 17, without the ship getting meaningfully bigger.

SSZ “Zero” — the one that actually won the war

If a cadet asks which SS mattered most, it is not the one you are briefing. The SS Zero was designed at a south‑coast station by three serving officers, not by a contractor, and they took an ordinary boat as the model for the car — keel, ribs, three‑ply sides, braced with piano wire. Over 70 were built; it became the standard SS.

Its endurance record is the kind of number that ends an argument: SSZ 39 flew a patrol of 50 hours 55 minutes. Others logged 30 h 20 m and a cluster between 25½ and 26½ hours, against a nominal 17‑hour design endurance and an average patrol of eight.

Whale's verdict on the type is also the doctrinal lesson of the whole class:

“A large fleet of airships of comparatively small capacity is of far more value for an anti‑submarine campaign than a lesser fleet of ships of infinitely greater capacity.” Whale (1919), p. 114

04The three cars — the fuselages

The three original SS versions differ in one component only: the car. Same mission, same envelope family, same rigging. Whale is explicit that “the system of rigging to all intents and purposes is the same in all types of S.S. ships, the suspensions being adjusted to suit the different makes of car.”

A distinction a sharp cadet will catch you on

Only one of the three was a genuine, literal aeroplane fuselage.

  • B.E.2c — a real B.E.2c fuselage, stripped. Actual aeroplane.
  • Armstrong Whitworth — a car designed by Armstrong Whitworth, closely similar to the B.E.2c arrangement, but built as an airship car.
  • Maurice Farman — not a Farman at all. It was built by Airships Ltd and is called “Maurice Farman” purely because it resembled a Maurice Farman aeroplane body. No Farman aircraft was cut up to make it.

So if someone asks “did they really hang three different aeroplanes under balloons?” the honest answer is: one was an aeroplane, one was an aeroplane‑maker's car, and one just looked like a Farman.

B.E.2c — the original car

The Royal Aircraft Factory B.E.2c was the obvious donor: it was already in mass production, it was stable to the point of docility (the quality that made it a death‑trap over the Western Front made it ideal for a loitering patrol), and its 75 hp Renault was a known quantity.

What was done to it, exactly:

  • Removed: wings, rudder, elevators, landing wheels, axle and suspension.
  • Added: two ash skids in place of the undercarriage, each carried on three struts.
  • Kept: the aeroplane's own control fittings — rudder bar under the feet, a vertical hand wheel for the elevators — so a pilot flew the airship with aeroplane controls.
  • Engine: 75 hp air‑cooled Renault V8, 438 lb, bore 96 mm × stroke 120 mm, driving a four‑bladed tractor propeller through 2:1 reduction gear.
  • Seating: observer/wireless operator forward of the pilot, behind the engine; pilot aft.

Maurice Farman — the pusher car

Built by Airships Ltd, resembling a Maurice Farman aeroplane body. The decisive difference is that it is a pusher: engine mounted aft, four‑bladed pusher propeller, petrol tanks forward feeding the carburettors by gravity.

It was slower — about 40 mph against the B.E.2c's 50 — and nobody minded, because it was far better to work in. Whale records that the loss of speed “was compensated for owing to the increased comfort provided for the crew, the cars being more roomy and suitable for airship work in every way.” On an eight‑hour patrol in a North Sea winter, roomy beats fast.

  • Seating: pilot in front, wireless operator behind — the reverse of the B.E.2c car. Several had a third seat for a passenger or engineer.
  • Dual controls for rudder and elevator, pilot and observer — a real safety feature none of the others had.
  • Engines: normally Renault; one famously fitted with a 75 hp Rolls‑Royce Hawk, which flew a patrol of 18 hours 20 minutes — more than double the type's nominal endurance.

Armstrong Whitworth — the long‑legged one

Armstrong Whitworth's car (the F.K. series, designed by Frederick Koolhoven) was a tractor layout “in all ways generally similar to the B.E.2c”, with two differences that matter:

  • Single landing skid with buffers, instead of the B.E.2c's twin ash skids — the quickest way to tell the two apart in a photograph.
  • Fuel slung from the envelope, not carried in the car. Two aluminium tanks hung in a fabric sling fed the carburettors through flexible pipes and a two‑way cock. That saved 100 lb against the B.E.2c installation — and 100 lb of saved weight is 100 lb of extra fuel, crew kit or bombs.

It needed the larger 70,000 ft³ envelope — its 100 hp water‑cooled Green engine was heavy, and on 60,000 ft³ “the margin of lift was decidedly small.” The payoff was 12 hours' endurance against the B.E.2c's 7–8.

The black ship

In the summer of 1916 an Armstrong Whitworth SS was rigged to an envelope doped black and sent to France for night operations — successfully. It is the ancestor of every blacked‑out night mission argument you will ever hear, and it is a genuinely unusual fact to have ready.

Honest caveat on these galleries

No photograph is known to this brief that shows a Maurice Farman or Armstrong Whitworth car actually slung under an SS envelope. The aircraft images above show the aeroplanes those cars derived from or resembled, which is what makes the shape recognisable. The one confirmed car‑under‑envelope image here is the B.E.2c type (Whale plate p.98, top of this document) plus the IWM car photograph. If you show a Farman aeroplane, say “this is the aeroplane the car resembled” — do not let it be taken as a photograph of the airship.

05Anatomy — every part, and what it is for

All of the following is from Whale's 1919 account, which describes the SS in engineering detail because he expected his readers to be airship men. These are the parts a cadet can ask about.

First principle: a blimp has no skeleton

The thing to understand before any part makes sense

An SS is a non‑rigid. There is no internal frame. The envelope holds its shape because the gas inside is at a slightly higher pressure than the air outside — it is a pressure vessel, like a football. Lose that pressure and the ship does not merely sink, it loses its shape, the nose blows in, and it stops being steerable. Almost every system described below exists to hold pressure constant as altitude and temperature change.

That is the whole design problem, and it is why “blimp” is a different machine from a Zeppelin rather than just a smaller one.

The envelope

Fabric
Four layers: two plies of rubber‑proofed fabric with a layer of rubber between them, plus a further rubber layer on the inner (gas) surface. The rubber is the gas barrier; the fabric carries the load.
Dope
Five coats, in a fixed order: two of Delta dope (flexible), two of aluminium dope, one of aluminium varnish. The aluminium is not decoration — it reflects sunlight, and sunlight heats the gas, raises the pressure and vents hydrogen overboard. Shiny skin = less gas lost.
Shape
Streamlined: blunt at the nose, tapering to the tail. 143 ft 6 in long, 27 ft 9 in maximum diameter on the 60,000 ft³ envelope.
Nose stiffening
24 canes in fabric pockets radiating from a point 2½ in ahead of the nose, under an aluminium conical cap and a fabric nose cap. Without it the nose blows inward at speed — the one place where air pressure beats gas pressure.

Ballonets — the single cleverest part

If you only explain one system, explain this one, because it is the thing nobody expects.

Inside the gasbag are two more bags — full of air, not hydrogen. One forward, one aft, 6,375 ft³ each. (The SS 1 prototype had only one; production ships had two. The SST had four.)

They solve this problem: as the ship climbs, outside pressure falls, so the hydrogen expands. With no ballonets you would have to vent precious gas every time you went up. Instead:

  • Climbing — hydrogen expands, squeezes the air ballonets, air is pushed out through the air valves. Envelope stays taut, no gas lost.
  • Descending — hydrogen contracts, ballonets are re‑inflated with air from the propeller slipstream. Envelope stays taut, no shape lost.
  • Trim — filling the forward or aft ballonet preferentially tilts the ship nose‑up or nose‑down.
Air scoop
A slanting aluminium tube taking air from the propeller draught to the underside of the envelope. It is free, continuous ram air whenever the engine is running — which is also why an engine failure is a pressure emergency, not just a propulsion one.
Air duct
A longitudinal fabric hose along the underside connecting both ballonet inlets to the scoop.
Crab‑pots
Non‑return fabric valves, one each side of the scoop junction. They let air in and refuse to let it back out, so one ballonet cannot empty into the other. The name is period slang and cadets love it — it works like a lobster pot.
Air valves
Two automatic, one per ballonet, on the underside. Set to open at 25–28 mm of water pressure. Note the detail: air pressure tends to push these valves open, so the spring is mounted inside the envelope to hold them shut.

Gas valves

Bottom valve
Automatic, spring set to open at 30–35 mm. Note it opens at a higher pressure than the air valves — the ship always dumps air before it will dump hydrogen. That ordering is a safety design, not an accident.
Top valve
Hand controlled only. The pilot pulls it to descend. Cords led to the pilot's seat through eyes on the envelope.
Construction
Two wooden rings clamp the envelope fabric, held by studs and butterfly nuts; an aluminium disc seats on a stretched indiarubber ring and is held shut by a compressed spring.

Rip panel — the emergency deflation

A 14 ft 5 in by ~8 in panel on top of the envelope, toward the nose. Pulling the ripping cord tears it open and dumps the entire gas content in seconds.

You use it when the ship is on the ground in a wind and about to be dragged or rolled. The overlap is sewn and taped so that in normal use it is as strong and gastight as any other part of the envelope — it is not a weak spot waiting to fail. The cord runs aft to a pulley above the car, then round the side of the envelope and in through a gland.

Rigging — how the car actually hangs

Whale calls the system “simplicity itself”, and it is tangential — the cables run tangent to the envelope curve rather than through it. The load is spread as a branching tree, so no single point on the fabric takes a concentrated pull:

“C” suspensions
Six per side, 25 cwt stranded steel cable. These are the main members carrying the car.
“B” bridles
Each C cable branches into two.
“A” bridles
Each B is supported at its ends by these — so one main suspension ends in four attachment points.
Eta patches
The attachment itself: a metal D‑fitting with webbing bands spread fanwise and glued to the envelope. Named for the airship Eta, where the method was developed. This is how you attach a steel cable to a fabric bag without tearing it.
Why it is built as a tree

Six main cables × four A‑bridle ends = the car's whole weight spread over 24 patches per side, along most of the envelope's length. Hang a 1,000 lb car off six points and you tear the fabric; hang it off forty‑eight and each patch carries about twenty pounds.

Ground handling and mooring

Handling guys
Four — two under the bow, two under the stern, each pair from a group of four Eta patches. These are what the ground party grabs. An SS needed a large landing party; the photographs of SSZ 17 at Pembroke show why.
Mooring point
The forward patch group is the anchoring point. A 25 cwt cable bridle runs from it to the forward ends of the car skids; the junction of that bridle and the skid cables is the mooring point, so strain is divided evenly between envelope and car rather than being taken by either alone.
Trail rope
120 ft of 2‑inch manila, coiled on the car side, dropped by a release gear.
Grapnel
On a short rope with an eye that slides down the trail rope and catches on a knot at the end.

Fins, rudders and elevators

Original layout
Four fins and rudders set radially — two horizontal, two below in an inverted‑V.
Revised layout
In many ships the two lower fins were dropped for a single vertical fin and rudder centrally underneath. If you are asked why: fewer surfaces to damage on landing, and less to foul the ground party.
Size
Three planes, each 16 ft × 8 ft 6 in, gross stabilising area 402½ ft². Original rudders 3 ft × 8 ft 6 in; with the single‑plane layout, 4‑ft rudders.
Construction
Spruce, aluminium and steel tubing, wire‑braced, covered in linen doped and varnished in position. Two steel kingposts per plane.
Attachment
Spruce skids fastened to the envelope by eight lacing patches, plus stay wires.

In the car

Controls
The aeroplane's own: steering by the feet (rudder bar), elevating by a vertical hand wheel mounted fore‑and‑aft across the seat. Control wires run aft inside the fuselage fairing to the extreme tail.
Instruments
Watch · air‑speed indicator in knots · aneroid to 10,000 ft · Elliott revolution counter · Clift inclinometer (±20°) · map case with celluloid front · oil pressure · petrol pressure · glass petrol level · two concentric glass gauges for gas pressure.
Compass
On a small wooden pedestal on the floor between the pilot's legs — as far from the engine's iron as the car allows.
Fuel
Three tanks: gravity and intermediate (from the aeroplane) plus a bottom tank under the front seat. Petrol forced up to the gravity tank by air pressure from a hand pump beside the pilot's seat. The oil tank sits inside the car ahead of the observer.
Ballast
14 gallons of water (140 lb) immediately behind the pilot's seat.
Wireless
Receiver and transmitter in the car on accumulator batteries, aerial reel outside. Range 50–60 miles — which is the entire point of the aircraft.
Armament
Bombs in frames about the centre of the undercarriage (up to 160 lb); bomb sight by the release gear outside the car to starboard; a Lewis gun on a post beside the pilot — not always carried on early SS ships.
The answer to “how did it actually kill submarines?”

Mostly, it didn't — and that was the point. Over the final 15 months of the war SS‑type ships flew 10,000+ patrols, ~1.5 million miles, 50,000+ hours. They sighted 49 U‑boats and 27 were attacked from the air or by ships. The number that matters is different: only one vessel was ever sunk while being escorted by an airship.

A submerged U‑boat is blind and slow. A visible airship overhead forces it down and keeps it there. The weapon was presence, and the wireless set mattered more than the bombs.

06The questions cadets will actually ask

Written as answers you can give out loud. Where the honest answer is “disputed” or “I don't know”, it says so — that is a stronger position in a room than a confident guess that someone can correct.

Is it SS for Submarine Scout or Sea Scout?

Both appear in the literature. Submarine Scout is the original and the operational name; Sea Scout shows up alongside it, especially in later writing. Named for the mission — scouting for submarines.

Why is it called a “class”?

Because in 1915 the Navy stopped numbering these ships in its single running airship list and started numbering them within their own class from 1 upward — SS, SSP, SS Zero, Coastal, C Star, North Sea. It was a deliberate administrative decision made because they were about to build a great many near‑identical ships. The rigids kept the old sequence, which is why their numbers jump from No. 1 to R 9 to R 23.

Is a blimp the same as a Zeppelin?

No, and this is the distinction most worth being crisp about. A Zeppelin is rigid — a metal skeleton with separate gas cells inside; it holds its shape with no gas at all. An SS is non‑rigid — no frame whatsoever. It holds its shape by internal gas pressure alone. Lose pressure and it loses its shape and its steering. Different machines, not different sizes.

What stops the gasbag from bursting as it climbs?

The ballonets — two air bags inside the hydrogen envelope, 6,375 ft³ each. Climbing, the hydrogen expands and squeezes air out of them through automatic valves set at 25–28 mm. Descending, they refill from the propeller slipstream through the air scoop. The envelope stays taut and no hydrogen is wasted. Filling them unevenly also trims the ship nose‑up or nose‑down.

What happens if the engine quits?

It is worse than it sounds, because the air scoop feeds off the propeller draught. No engine means no ram air into the ballonets, so a descent can no longer be compensated by refilling them — it becomes a pressure problem as well as a propulsion problem. That is precisely why the SST “Twin” was developed: Whale says the idea was “to render the small type of airship less liable to loss from engine failure.”

Why hydrogen? Wasn't that known to be dangerous?

Yes, and they used it anyway because Britain had no practical alternative in 1915. Helium was effectively a United States monopoly and was not available in militarily useful quantities to anyone else during the First World War. Hydrogen was also cheaper, could be produced domestically, and gives roughly 8% more lift.

The mitigation was operational rather than technical: no smoking, careful engine and exhaust placement, bonding, and the rip panel to dump gas before a ground accident turned into a fire.

Then why did the Hindenburg still use hydrogen in 1937, after all the accidents?

Because the one country with helium would not sell it. This is the best version of the answer, and it surprises people:

  • LZ 129 Hindenburg was designed for helium. Zeppelin knew exactly how dangerous hydrogen was.
  • The United States had a near‑total world monopoly on helium, from natural gas fields in the Texas panhandle.
  • US law — the Helium Act of 1925 and the Helium Control Act of 1927 — made it a strategic mineral and barred export.
  • Germany built the ship gambling it could obtain an export licence. The National Munitions Control Board refused, and with the Nazi government in power after 1933 there was no appetite in Washington to reconsider. Zeppelin had to re‑engineer a finished helium airship to run on hydrogen.
  • Grimly: designed around heavier helium, the ship had surplus lift on hydrogen — which the company spent on extra passenger cabins.

And they had real evidence it could be done safely. LZ 127 Graf Zeppelin, 1928–1937, on hydrogen: 590 flights, ~1.1 million miles, 144 ocean crossings, 13,110 passengers, and not one passenger or crewman injured.

The line to use:

Everyone had learned the lesson. Only the Americans had the gas.

And the follow‑up to be ready for: hydrogen was the fuel, but what ignited it has never been definitively established — static discharge, St Elmo's fire and the skin's doped coating have all been argued. Don't be pushed into naming a settled cause.

Why did America use helium when Britain and Germany didn't?

Because America had it and they had already paid for the lesson. The Roma — entry 02 of the main dossier — crashed in 1922 on hydrogen, killing 34. The US Navy went all‑helium afterwards and never went back: Akron, Macon, and the L‑, K‑ and ZPG‑classes are all helium ships because of it.

How many were built, and how many versions were there?

About 158 of all SS versions and variants. Of those roughly 60 were the original three car‑types (B.E.2c, Maurice Farman, Armstrong Whitworth); the SS Zero alone accounted for 70‑plus. Then SSP (6), SST and the experimental SSE. Each cost about £2,500.

Did they actually sink any submarines?

Very few — and that is the wrong measure. In the last 15 months of the war SS‑types flew over 10,000 patrols, ~1.5 million miles, 50,000+ hours, sighted 49 U‑boats and attacked or cued attacks on 27. The decisive figure is that only one ship was ever sunk while under airship escort. A submerged U‑boat is blind and slow; an airship overhead forces it under and keeps it there. The weapon was presence, and the wireless mattered more than the bombs.

How long could it stay up?

Design endurance ran from 7–8 hours (SS B.E.2c) to 17 hours (SSZ and SST). Reality beat it badly. A Hawk‑engined Maurice Farman flew 18 h 20 m; the record is SSZ 39 at 50 hours 55 minutes, with others at 30 h 20 m and a cluster around 25½–26½ h. Average patrol was about eight hours.

How high could it go?

Service ceiling about 5,000 ft, which was plenty — you want to be low enough to see into the water. The British airship altitude record was set by an SS B.E.2c at 10,300 ft in the summer of 1916, double its normal ceiling.

Who was on board, and what did they do?

Normally two. The pilot steered and ran the engine — no separate engineer. The observer doubled as the wireless operator, with the set around his seat and the aerial reel outside the car. The Maurice Farman car sometimes carried a third for a passenger or engineer and had dual rudder and elevator controls. Later SSP and SSZ cars seated three; the SST, five.

How do you land something with no brakes and no wheels?

You don't, really — a ground party catches it. The ship trails a 120 ft manila trail rope dropped by release gear; handlers grab that and the four handling guys under bow and stern and walk it down. Landing gear is ash skids, not wheels. If the wind got up and the ship was about to be dragged, the pilot pulled the rip panel and dumped the gas deliberately.

What's a crab-pot?

A non‑return fabric valve in the air duct either side of the scoop junction — period slang, works like a lobster pot: air in, not back out. It stops one ballonet emptying into the other.

What's an Eta patch?

How you attach a steel cable to a fabric bag without tearing it. A metal D‑fitting with webbing bands spread fanwise and glued to the envelope. Named after the airship Eta, where the technique was developed.

Why did they use an aeroplane fuselage instead of designing a car?

Time. Fisher said “at once” on 28 February 1915 and the first ship was in service on 18 March — under three weeks. B.E.2cs were already in mass production, and the factories and skills existed. By 1916 they did design proper cars (SSP, then the boat‑hulled SSZ) — as soon as there was time to.

If someone asks something I don't know?

Say so, then say where the answer lives. “I don't have that figure — the standard source is Whale's 1919 British Airships, and I can look it up.” This brief is built from a period source precisely so you can name it. A confident wrong number is the only real failure mode here.
